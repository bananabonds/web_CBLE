1. Fluid Flow

A thin liquid film running down a vertical surface is the simplest case in which the flow rate is set by a Reynolds number. The film Reynolds number uses the mass flow per unit width of the surface:

Refilm = 4 Γμ = 4 ρ Qμ b

The factor of 4 comes from the hydraulic diameter of an open-channel film, which is four times the film thickness. The regime boundaries are Re < 20 for laminar wavy-free flow, 20 < Re < 1 500 for laminar wavy flow, and Re > 1 500 for turbulent film flow. Most textbook problems sit in the laminar range.

The relation between flow rate and film thickness follows from the Nusselt analysis:

δ = ( 3 μ Γρ² g )1/3

The calculation sequence for a film problem is: read Re → solve for Γ → divide by ρ to get volumetric flow per unit width → convert the units. The unit conversion from ft²/hr to gal/ft·hr uses 1 ft³ = 7.48 gal, which is the single most important conversion in this calculation.

Worked 1.1: flow rate of a falling water film at Re = 10

Water runs down a vertical plate with a film Reynolds number of 10. Find the flow rate in gal/ft·hr. Take the density of water as 62.4 lb/ft³ and the viscosity as 1 cP = 2.42 lb/ft·hr.

Show solution
Step 1 — write the film Reynolds number definition and solve for Γ
Refilm = 4 Γμ ⇒ Γ = Re μ4
Step 2 — substitute the given values
Γ = 10 × 2.424 = 6.05 lb/ft·hr
Step 3 — convert mass flow per unit width to volumetric flow per unit width
Qb = Γρ = 6.0562.4 = 0.09695 ft2/hr
Step 4 — convert to gallons per foot per hour (1 ft³ = 7.48 gal)
Qb = 0.09695 × 7.48 = 0.725 gal/ft·hr ≈ 0.727
The answer 0.277 comes from omitting the factor of 4 in the Reynolds number definition — using Re = Γ/μ instead of Re = 4Γ/μ. That missing factor of 4 propagates through to give a flow rate one quarter as large. The 0.727 key matches the calculation to within the precision of the viscosity conversion.
Variables Refilm — film Reynolds number, 10 Γ — mass flow per unit width of plate, lb/ft·hr μ — viscosity, 2.42 lb/ft·hr (1 cP) ρ — density, 62.4 lb/ft³ Q/b — volumetric flow per unit width, gal/ft·hr
Concept discourse: why the factor of 4 appearsThe hydraulic diameter of an open-channel film is Dh = 4 × (cross-section) / (wetted perimeter). For a film of thickness δ on a plate of width b, the cross-section is bδ and the wetted perimeter is b + 2δ ≈ b. So Dh ≈ 4δ. The Reynolds number is then ρvDh/μ = 4ρvδ/μ = 4Γ/μ, with Γ = ρvδ. The 4 is the four times the film thickness — nothing else. Writing the derivation once makes the factor unforgettable.

2. Heat Transfer Coefficient

The heat transfer coefficient measures the rate at which heat crosses a fluid boundary per unit area per degree of temperature difference. For a solid wall of thickness L and conductivity k, the conductive resistance is L/k. For a fluid boundary, the convective resistance is 1/h. In series they add:

q = ΔTΣ R, Rcond = Lk, Rconv = 1h

For a composite wall with layers in series and convection on both sides,

q = T1 − T21/h1 + Σ(L/k) + 1/h2

The temperature at any interface follows by subtracting the resistances upstream of that interface from the total ΔT.

For a flat plate in forced convection the heat transfer coefficient is obtained from the Nusselt number correlation:

Nu = h Dk, Nu = 0.023 Re0.8 Pr0.4 (turbulent, 0.7 < Pr < 160)

The Prandtl number is

Pr = μ cpk

The L/D ratio affects h only in the entrance region; beyond L/D ≈ 60 (or Re > 10 000 in the more common criterion) the effect vanishes and the fully developed correlations apply.

3. Heat Exchanger

Heat exchangers are compared by the log-mean temperature difference and the rate equation Q = U A ΔTlm. The terminal temperatures depend on the flow arrangement:

counterflow: ΔT1 = Th,in − Tc,out, ΔT2 = Th,out − Tc,in
parallel flow: ΔT1 = Th,in − Tc,in, ΔT2 = Th,out − Tc,out
ΔTlm = ΔT1 − ΔT2ln( ΔT1 / ΔT2 )

The LMTD is always smaller than the arithmetic mean of the terminal differences. For a pure counterflow unit it is larger than for a pure parallel-flow unit for the same four temperatures; a multi-pass exchanger lies between, and the correction factor F (< 1) is applied to the counterflow LMTD:

Q = U A F ΔTlm,counter

When one flow rate is unknown, the rate equation is written twice — once from the hot-stream duty and once from the cold-stream duty — and the two are set equal. When a driving force is specified as the arithmetic mean, use (ΔT1 + ΔT2)/2 directly in place of the LMTD.

Worked 3.1: counterflow area for cooling water

20 kg/s of water is cooled from 360 K to 335 K in a counterflow exchanger by 25 kg/s of cold water entering at 300 K. The overall coefficient is 2 kW/m²·K. Take cp = 4.18 kJ/kg·K. Find the area required.

Show solution
Step 1 — heat duty from the hot stream
Q = mh cp ( Th,in − Th,out ) = 20 × 4.18 × 25 = 2 090 kW
Step 2 — cold-stream outlet temperature from the same duty
Tc,out = 300 + 2 09025 × 4.18 = 300 + 20 = 320 K
Step 3 — terminal differences in counterflow
ΔT1 = 360 − 320 = 40 K, ΔT2 = 335 − 300 = 35 K
Step 4 — log-mean temperature difference
ΔTlm = 40 − 35ln( 40 / 35 ) = 50.1335 = 37.45 K
Step 5 — area from Q = U A ΔTlm
A = 2 0902 × 37.45 = 27.9 m2 ≈ 28 m²
The answer 32 m² comes from using a larger LMTD or a slip in the duty. The LMTD here (37.45 K) is close to the arithmetic mean (37.5 K) because the terminal differences are close; this is not always true.

Concept discourse: why the multi-pass exchanger sits between counterflow and parallel flowA pure counterflow unit has the largest mean driving force for a given set of terminal temperatures, because the two streams move in opposite directions and the temperature difference between them stays relatively constant along the length. A pure parallel-flow unit has the smallest, because the two streams approach each other and the driving force collapses toward the exit. A multi-pass exchanger (tubes making several passes through a shell) is a mix of counterflow and parallel-flow behaviour: part of the flow is counter to the other stream and part is parallel. Its mean driving force is therefore between the two extremes, and the correction factor F (always less than 1) is applied to the counterflow LMTD. That is why the multi-pass area is always between the counterflow area and the parallel-flow area, not equal to either.

4. Evaporation

Evaporation of a liquid into a gas in which the gas itself is stagnant is the diffusion problem of a single component through a non-diffusing second component. The flux is not Fick's law with the concentration gradient alone; a convective term arises because the bulk mixture must move to carry the evaporating molecules away:

NA = D PR T z ln P − pA2P − pA1 = D PR T z pA1 − pA2pBM

Here pA1 is the vapour pressure at the liquid surface (saturation at the liquid temperature) and pA2 is the vapour partial pressure in the bulk gas (zero for dry air). The log-mean partial pressure of the inert gas is

pBM = pB2 − pB1ln( pB2 / pB1 ), pB = P − pA

The total evaporation from N vents of cross-sectional area A is N × NA × A. Converting the flux (mol/m²·s) to a mass rate (kg/day) requires the molar mass and the conversion factor 86 400 s/day.

Worked 4.1: evaporation loss from vented irrigation ditch

Water at 25 °C flows in a covered irrigation ditch. Every 100 m a vent line of 30 mm inside diameter and 1 m long connects to dry outside air at 25 °C. There are 10 vents in 1 000 m. Find the total evaporation loss in kg/day.

Show solution

Step 2 — inert-gas partial pressures at the two ends
pB1 = 101.325 − 3.169 = 98.156 kPa
pB2 = 101.325 − 0 = 101.325 kPa
Step 3 — log-mean inert partial pressure
pBM = 101.325 − 98.156ln( 101.325 / 98.156 ) = 3.1690.03178 = 99.71 kPa
Step 4 — diffusivity of water vapour in air at 25 °C
D = 2.6×10−5 m²/s
Step 5 — flux from the stagnant-film equation

Step 6 — total vent area

Step 7 — molar rate and mass rate

5. Mass Transfer Coefficient

The mass transfer coefficient kc (or kG, ky) relates the flux to a concentration or partial-pressure driving force:

NA = kc ( CA,b − CA,i ) = kG ( pA,b − pA,i ) = ky ( yb − yi )

The units of k depend on the driving force used: m/s for concentration, kmol/(m²·s·kPa) for partial pressure, kmol/(m²·s·Δy) for mole fraction. A number given without its units is incomplete. A number quoted as 7.4×10⁻³ with diffusivity in m²/s and length in m must carry the units m/s.

For diffusion through a stagnant film the coefficient is

kc = Dz

and for the stagnant-film correction the coefficient becomes

kG = D PR T z pBM

For forced convection over a flat plate the coefficient is obtained from the Sherwood number:

Sh = kc LD, Sh = 0.664 Re1/2 Sc1/3 (laminar)

with Sc = μ/(ρD). The diffusivity itself is estimated from the Fuller correlation:

D = 1.00×10−3 T1.75 √( 1/MA + 1/MB )P [ (Σv)A1/3 + (Σv)B1/3 ]²

with P in atm, D in cm²/s, and the Σv the diffusion volumes from the Fuller table.

Worked 5.1: effect of pressure on absorption rate

By what percentage is the rate of absorption of ammonia from a 10 % NH₃/air mixture into pure water changed when the total pressure is increased from 100 to 200 kPa? Assume all resistance lies in the gas phase.

Show solution
Step 1 — the stagnant-film flux for a gas-phase-controlled process
NA = D PR T z pBM ( pA,b − pA,i )
Step 2 — the diffusivity for gases varies inversely with pressure at fixed temperature (D P = constant)
D ∝ 1P
Step 3 — the driving force pA,b − pA,i scales linearly with P, because y is fixed by the gas composition
pA,b − pA,i ∝ P
Step 4 — the log-mean term pBM also scales with P, because every partial pressure scales with P
pBM ∝ P
Step 5 — assemble the pressure dependence of NA
NA ∝ (1/P) × PP × P = constant
NA is independent of pressure ⇒ 0 % change
The D P product is constant, the driving force doubles, and the log-mean doubles — so the three pressure factors cancel exactly and the flux is unchanged. This is a subtle result that is easy to miss. The answer 50 % comes from keeping the driving force fixed (wrong) or from an incomplete cancellation.
Variables D — diffusivity, m²/s P — total pressure, kPa pA,b, pA,i — bulk and interface partial pressures, kPa pBM — log-mean inert partial pressure, kPa NA — molar flux, kmol/m²·s

Concept discourse: units are the answerTwo questions in this set ask only for the units of a mass transfer coefficient or an evaporation rate. Writing the defining equation and letting the units of every right-hand-side quantity determine the units of the left-hand side is the only reliable approach — no memorizing of unit lists required.

6. Gas Absorption

The inert-free basis is the foundation. The carrier gas does not absorb, so its flow is constant, and the solute is tracked as a mole ratio:

Y = y1 − y, X = x1 − x

The equilibrium line for Henry's law is

Y* = m X (dilute)

and the operating line is

G ( Y − Ytop ) = L ( X − Xtop )

The minimum liquid rate is set at the pinch, which for a straight equilibrium line and a pure solvent is at the bottom of the tower:

( LG )min = Yin − YoutX*out, X*out = Yinm

The number of theoretical stages for a straight equilibrium line follows the Kremser equation:

N = ln[ ( Yin − mXin )/( Yout − mXin ) ( 1 − 1/A ) + 1/A ]ln A, A = LmG

The absorption factor A is L/(mG), not L/G; the equilibrium slope m must be divided out. For absorption, the operating line lies above the equilibrium line (the driving force y − y* is positive); for stripping it lies below.

For the ratio of the stagnant-film flux to the equimolar flux:

NA,UMDNA,EMD = 1( 1 − y )lm

which is always greater than 1.

Concept discourse: absorption factor is L/(mG), not L/GThe absorption factor A = L/(mG) has m in the denominator because the equilibrium line slope sets how much liquid is needed per unit of gas. When m is small (soluble gas), A is large and fewer stages are needed; when m is large (insoluble gas), A is small and many stages are needed. Using L/G directly as the absorption factor ignores this and produces a stage count that is off by a factor of 2 or more. The m in the denominator is the entire point of the Kremser equation.

7. Tower Height

A packed tower's height is the product of the number of transfer units and the height of a transfer unit:

Z = HOG × NOG

where HOG is the height of an overall gas-phase transfer unit and NOG is the number of overall gas-phase transfer units. The number of transfer units follows from the driving-force integral:

NOG = ∫y2y1 dyy − y*

For a straight equilibrium line and a straight operating line, the integral is approximated by the log-mean driving force:

NOG = y1 − y2Δylm, Δylm = Δy1 − Δy2ln( Δy1 / Δy2 )

with Δy1 = y1 − y*1 at the bottom and Δy2 = y2 − y*2 at the top.

The height of an overall gas-phase transfer unit is

HOG = GKy a P

in molar units, or, when the individual coefficients are known,

HOG = Gky a + m Gkx a

where kya and kxa are the volumetric gas-film and liquid-film coefficients. The first term is the gas-film contribution; the second is the liquid-film contribution weighted by the equilibrium slope m.

The superficial gas velocity vs sets the column diameter: Ac = Qv/vs. The given Qv is at a specified temperature and pressure and must be the inlet value.

Concept discourse: NTU × HTU is the whole of packed-tower designThe design of a packed tower is a two-step procedure. First, the number of transfer units NOG comes from the mass balance and the equilibrium line — it is a thermodynamic quantity, independent of the packing. Second, the height of a transfer unit HOG comes from the mass-transfer coefficients — it is a hydrodynamic quantity that depends on the packing, the flow rates and the fluid properties. The product gives the height. This separation matters because it tells you which knob to turn: NOG is fixed by the separation specification, and only HOG can be improved by choosing a better packing or changing the flow rates.
