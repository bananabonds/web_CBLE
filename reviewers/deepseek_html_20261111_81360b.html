1. Slope of Line

The slope of a line through two points is the ratio of the vertical change to the horizontal change:

m = y2 − y1x2 − x1

Three forms of the line equation follow directly:

FormEquationUse
Point-slopey − y1 = m(x − x1)when a point and the slope are known
Slope-intercepty = mx + bwhen the y-intercept is known
GeneralAx + By + C = 0when the equation is given in this form

Two lines are parallel if their slopes are equal, and perpendicular if the product of their slopes is −1:

parallel: m1 = m2
perpendicular: m1 m2 = −1 ⇒ m2 = −1m1

The distance from a point (x0, y0) to a line Ax + By + C = 0 is

d = | A x0 + B y0 + C |√( A² + B² )

The absolute value in the numerator makes the distance positive regardless of which side of the line the point lies on; dropping it is the commonest sign error.

The angle between two lines of slopes m1 and m2 is

tan θ = m2 − m11 + m1 m2

with θ the acute angle. The formula uses the absolute value of the difference in practice; the sign indicates which line is steeper.

Collinearity of three points means the slopes between each pair are equal:

( y2 − y1 ) / ( x2 − x1 ) = ( y3 − y1 ) / ( x3 − x1 )

For points whose y-coordinates are in arithmetic progression, the x-coordinates must also be in arithmetic progression: if y1, y2, y3 are equally spaced, then x2 − x1 = x3 − x2.

2. Triangle

A triangle in analytic geometry is described by the coordinates of its vertices. Three quantities recur: its area, its centroid, and whether its three points are collinear.

Area of a triangle from coordinates

The shoelace formula gives the area of a triangle with vertices (x1, y1), (x2, y2), (x3, y3):

A = 12 | x1( y2 − y3 ) + x2( y3 − y1 ) + x3( y1 − y2 ) |

The absolute value handles the sign that comes from the winding order. The formula works for any triangle and does not require the triangle to be right-angled or aligned with the axes.

Collinearity

Three points are collinear if and only if the area of the triangle they form is zero:

x1( y2 − y3 ) + x2( y3 − y1 ) + x3( y1 − y2 ) = 0

Equivalently, the slopes between the pairs of points are equal. The area formula is the more robust test because it does not fail when the line is vertical.

The centroid (previewed here, developed in Chapter 6)

The centroid of a triangle is the average of the three vertices:

( x̄, ȳ ) = ( x1 + x2 + x33, y1 + y2 + y33 )

It is the point where the three medians meet, and it divides each median in the ratio 2:1 from the vertex.

3. Circle

A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance (the radius) from a fixed point (the center). Three equation forms cover the chapter.

Standard forms

center (h, k), radius r: ( x − h )² + ( y − k )² = r²
expanded: x² + y² + Dx + Ey + F = 0

The expanded form is converted back to center-radius form by completing the square:

x² + Dx = ( x + D/2 )² − D²/4
y² + Ey = ( y + E/2 )² − E²/4

After completing both squares, the center is (−D/2, −E/2) and the radius is √(D²/4 + E²/4 − F).

Circles from a diameter

If the endpoints of a diameter are (x1, y1) and (x2, y2), the center is the midpoint and the radius is half the distance:

center = ( x1 + x22, y1 + y22 )
r = 12 √( ( x2 − x1 )² + ( y2 − y1 )² )

The resulting equation can be multiplied through by 4 to clear the fraction in r², which is why the answers in the exam are often written with 4x² + 4y² as the leading terms.

Tangent to a circle from a point

A line is tangent to a circle if the perpendicular distance from the center to the line equals the radius. That condition gives a single equation in the unknown slope or constant.

distance( center, line ) = r

Chord common to two circles

Two circles intersect in a chord whose equation is found by subtracting their two equations:

( x² + y² + D1x + E1y + F1 ) − ( x² + y² + D2x + E2y + F2 ) = 0

The x² and y² terms cancel, leaving a straight line — the radical axis. The chord length then follows by finding the distance from the center of one circle to that line and using the Pythagorean relation:

chord length = 2 √( r² − d² )

with d the perpendicular distance from the center to the radical axis.

4. Center

The center of a conic is the point about which the conic is symmetric. For a circle, it is the point equidistant from all points on the circumference. For an ellipse or hyperbola, it is the midpoint of the segment joining the two foci and also the midpoint of the major (or transverse) axis.

Finding the center by completing the square

Most center questions present the equation of a circle or conic in expanded form. Completing the square in both x and y converts it to a form from which the center can be read off:

x² + Dx + y² + Ey + F = 0
( x + D/2 )² + ( y + E/2 )² = D²/4 + E²/4 − F

The center is

( h, k ) = ( −D/2, −E/2 )

Note the double negative: the sign of the center's coordinate is the opposite of the sign of the linear coefficient. This is where most sign errors occur.

Center of an ellipse or hyperbola

The same completion of the square applies, but with coefficients on the squared terms:

Ax² + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0

After dividing through by A and C and completing the squares, the center is still (−D/2A, −E/2C). A quick way to read the center from the general form is to solve

∂F/∂x = 0 and ∂F/∂y = 0

simultaneously; for a conic, these two equations are linear in x and y and their solution is the center.

Parabola

A parabola does not have a center in the same sense; it has a vertex. The vertex of a parabola written in standard form y = ax² + bx + c is at

x = −b2a

and the latus rectum has length |4p| where p is the focal distance. The latus rectum of y² = 4px is 4|p|; for a parabola written as (y − k)² = 4p(x − h), the coefficient of (x − h) is 4p, so the latus rectum is |coefficient|.

5. Eccentricity

The eccentricity e measures how far a conic deviates from a circle. It is defined as the ratio of the distance from a point on the conic to the focus to the distance from that point to the directrix:

e = distance to focusdistance to directrix

Its value identifies the conic:

eConic
e = 0circle
0 < e < 1ellipse
e = 1parabola
e > 1hyperbola

Eccentricity of an ellipse

For an ellipse with semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b, the focal distance c satisfies c² = a² − b², and the eccentricity is

e = ca = √( 1 − b²a² )

For an ellipse, 0 < e < 1. A circle has e = 0; a highly elongated ellipse has e close to 1.

Eccentricity of a hyperbola

For a hyperbola with transverse semi-axis a and conjugate semi-axis b, the focal distance c satisfies c² = a² + b², and

e = ca = √( 1 + b²a² )

For a hyperbola, e > 1. The plus sign in the square root is what distinguishes the hyperbola's eccentricity formula from the ellipse's.

Eccentricity of a conic from the discriminant

For the general second-degree equation Ax² + Bxy + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0, the discriminant B² − 4AC tells the type of conic:

B² − 4AC < 0 ⇒ ellipse (or circle)
B² − 4AC = 0 ⇒ parabola
B² − 4AC > 0 ⇒ hyperbola

The Bxy term's presence rotates the conic; even with B ≠ 0 the discriminant test classifies the shape, but the axes are tilted relative to the coordinate axes.

6. Centroid

The centroid (or center of gravity) of a triangle is the point where the three medians intersect. It has a simple coordinate formula:

( x̄, ȳ ) = ( x1 + x2 + x33, y1 + y2 + y33 )

The centroid divides each median in the ratio 2:1, with the longer part on the vertex side. That means the centroid is two-thirds of the way from a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side:

centroid = vertex + 23 ( midpoint of opposite side − vertex )

Centroid of a composite shape

When a figure is divided into simple pieces, the centroid is the area-weighted average of the pieces' centroids:

x̄ = Σ Ai x̄iΣ Ai, ȳ = Σ Ai ȳiΣ Ai

The formula also works for a polygon given by its vertices; the shoelace formula for area and a related moment formula give the centroid of any polygon.

Relation to other triangle centers

For a triangle the four classical centers have distinct roles:

CenterCoordinates from vertices
Centroidaverage of the three vertices
Incenterweighted average of the vertices by opposite side lengths
Circumcenterintersection of the perpendicular bisectors
Orthocenterintersection of the altitudes

For an equilateral triangle all four coincide. For any other triangle they are four distinct points, and confusing the centroid with the incenter or circumcenter is the standard error in questions that ask for a specific center.

Worked 6.1: centroid of a triangle

Find the centroid of the triangle whose vertices are (2, 3), (−4, 6) and (2, −6).

Show solution
Step 1 — apply the centroid formula to the x-coordinates
x̄ = 2 + ( −4 ) + 23 = 03 = 0
Step 2 — apply to the y-coordinates
ȳ = 3 + 6 + ( −6 )3 = 33 = 1

The answer (0, −1) comes from a sign slip in the y-sum: 3 + 6 − 6 = 3, not −3. The three y-coordinates are 3, 6, and −6; the negative sign belongs only to the third, not to the sum. Adding carefully is the whole problem.
Variables (2, 3), (−4, 6), (2, −6) — the three vertices (x̄, ȳ) — the centroid
Worked 6.2: centroid by the 2:1 division

Find the centroid of the triangle with vertices A(1, 2), B(7, 4), C(3, 8) using the median-division property.

Show solution
Step 1 — midpoint of the side opposite vertex A, which is BC
MBC = ( 7 + 32, 4 + 82 ) = ( 5, 6 )
Step 2 — centroid is 2/3 of the way from A to M
x̄ = 1 + 23 ( 5 − 1 ) = 1 + 83 = 113 ≈ 3.667
ȳ = 2 + 23 ( 6 − 2 ) = 2 + 83 = 143 ≈ 4.667
Step 3 — confirm with the average formula
x̄ = 1 + 7 + 33 = 113, ȳ = 2 + 4 + 83 = 143 ✓
centroid = (11/3, 14/3) ≈ (3.67, 4.67)
Both methods give the same answer, which is the point — the centroid formula is just the 2:1 division applied three times. The 2:1 ratio is from the vertex side: the centroid is closer to the midpoint than to the vertex, so the factor is 2/3 from the vertex and 1/3 from the midpoint.
Variables A, B, C — vertices of the triangle MBC — midpoint of side BC (x̄, ȳ) — centroid
