1. Laplace Transform

The Laplace transform turns a differential equation in time into an algebraic equation in the complex variable s. Three operations cover the whole chapter.

Transform of a derivative

L{ y′ } = s Y(s) − y(0)
L{ y″ } = s² Y(s) − s y(0) − y′(0)

Every initial condition must appear. A first-order equation brings one y(0); a second-order equation brings y(0) and y′(0), with the y(0) term multiplied by s. Omitting the initial-condition terms is the standard error, and it is easy to spot: if the transformed equation does not contain y(0) anywhere, the derivative rule has not been applied.

The final value theorem

limt→∞ y(t) = lims→0 s Y(s)

The theorem applies only when the poles of sY(s) lie strictly in the left half-plane, apart from a simple pole at s = 0. If sY(s) has a pole at a positive real value, or a pair of poles on the imaginary axis, the time function does not settle and the limit does not exist.

Reading the time-domain solution from the roots

For a linear constant-coefficient ODE with a constant forcing, the shape of the solution is decided entirely by the roots of the characteristic polynomial.

r = 0 (simple)constantsteady state

r = α ± βieαt( C1 cos βt + C2 sin βt )oscillation; decays if α < 0

Show solution
Step 1 — transform the left side term by term; the derivative carries y(0)
L{ 3 y′ } = 3 [ s Y(s) − y(0) ] = 3 [ s Y(s) − 2 ]
Step 2 — transform the algebraic term
L{ 6y } = 6 Y(s)
Step 3 — transform the constant forcing
L{ 9 } = 9s
Step 4 — assemble
3( s Y(s) − 2 ) + 6 Y(s) = 9s
The answer without the −2 is the transform of the homogeneous problem with zero initial condition, not of the problem as stated. The initial condition is not a decoration; it changes the algebra. Once the expression is written, Y(s) can be isolated for the inverse transform: Y(s) = (9/s + 6)/(3s + 6).

Show solution

conditions satisfied ✓

2. Process Response

The dynamic behavior of a process is described by its transfer function. Three parameters define most models, and three relations connect them to the step response.

First-order processes

G(s) = Kτ s + 1

For a step of magnitude M applied at t = 0, starting from y(0) = 0:

y(t) = K M ( 1 − e−t/τ )

The steady-state gain K is the final change in output divided by the step magnitude. The time constant τ is the time to reach 63.2 % of the final change.

Second-order processes

G(s) = Kτ² s² + 2 ζ τ s + 1

The damping ratio ζ decides the shape:

ζBehaviour
ζ > 1overdamped — no overshoot, slow approach
ζ = 1critically damped — fastest approach without overshoot
0 < ζ < 1underdamped — overshoots, oscillates, settles
ζ = 0undamped — sustained oscillation

For an underdamped response the overshoot and decay ratio are read from the peaks:

OS = peak − finalfinal, DR = second peak − finalfirst peak − final

First-order plus dead time (FOPTD)

G(s) = K e−θsτ s + 1

The tangent-line method reads the parameters from a step-test curve: draw the tangent at the inflection point, and read where it crosses the baseline (that time is θ) and where it reaches the final value line (that time minus θ is τ).

Physical realizability

A transfer function is physically realizable only if the degree of the numerator does not exceed the degree of the denominator:

m ≤ n (realizable), m > n (not realizable)

A numerator of higher degree than the denominator implies a response that anticipates the input — an impossible behaviour for a real process.

Dead time and the right-half-plane zero

A plug-flow pipe with volume V and flow q introduces a pure time delay:

G(s) = e−θs, θ = Vq

A zero at s = +a in the right half-plane produces inverse response: the output initially moves in the opposite direction to the input before settling to the correct final value.

Worked 2.7: a right-half-plane zero

A process has a transfer function with a zero at s = +2 (in the right half-plane). A positive step change is applied to its input. Describe the initial behaviour of the output.

Show solution
Step 1 — a right-half-plane zero gives a term in the response of the form (1 − a e+t/τ) rather than (1 − a e−t/τ)
the exponential grows rather than decays in the transient
Step 2 — at small t the growing term dominates and pushes the output in the direction opposite to the eventual steady state
the output initially decreases
Step 3 — at large t the other terms dominate and the output settles to its correct positive final value
The output initially decreases, then reverses and settles at a positive steady-state value
This is called inverse response. It is not an oscillation — the output does not cross zero repeatedly, it simply starts off in the wrong direction before turning around. The physical origin is a competing fast and slow path through the process; the fast path initially dominates and pushes the output the wrong way. The answer “oscillates symmetrically” describes an underdamped second-order response, which is a different phenomenon.

3. Control Loops

A control loop connects sensors, controllers and final control elements to hold a process variable at its set point. The architecture questions in the exam are mostly conceptual, and a small vocabulary of standard terms covers them.

Classification of variables

The inputs to a process are divided into two classes:

  • Manipulated variables — the inputs the control system can adjust (valve position, pump speed, heater power).
  • Disturbances — the inputs the control system cannot adjust (feed composition, ambient temperature, upstream pressure).

The outputs are the controlled variables, the ones held at their set points. The phrase “input variables” refers to manipulated variables and disturbances, not to controlled variables, which are outputs.

Simple control modes

Two binary-style modes appear in the questions.

ModeActionWhen used
On/off (two-position)Output is either fully on or fully off; the valve is either open or closedSlow cycling is acceptable — the equipment can start and stop, or a valve can open and close, without harm
Split-rangeOne controller output is split to drive two or more manipulated variables in sequenceOne output variable is controlled by several manipulated variables, each taking over in its own range

On/off control is the simplest and cheapest mode. Its disadvantage is that it always cycles, because the process must cross the set point before the controller switches. If the cycling is slow enough for the equipment, it is perfectly acceptable; if it is not, a continuous mode (P, PI, PID) is needed.

Multivariable systems and the RGA

Most industrial processes have several controlled variables and several manipulated variables. The question is which manipulated variable should drive which controlled variable — the pairing problem. The most widely used tool is the relative gain array (RGA):

λij = open-loop gain from uj to yiclosed-loop gain from uj to yi (all other loops closed)

A pairing with λ close to 1 is preferred; a pairing with λ close to 0 is to be avoided; a negative λ indicates that the loop will be unstable or strongly interacting. The RGA is computed from the steady-state gain matrix of the process.

When the process is genuinely noninteracting — each input affects exactly one output and no others — the RGA is the identity matrix and the pairing is trivial. The wrong answer “interacting because MIMO” confuses the number of inputs with the amount of interaction.

Steady-state optimization

Steady-state optimization is the real-time evaluation of the process operating conditions and the adjustment of the controlled variables to meet business objectives — minimizing cost, maximizing throughput, or meeting a product specification. It sits above the regulatory control layer: the regulatory layer holds variables at their set points, and the optimization layer decides what those set points should be.

Discrete measurements

A discrete (on/off) measurement indicates only whether a threshold has been crossed. A level switch that reports “liquid present” at a specific height is discrete. A continuous measurement gives a numerical value across a range — an oxygen analyser reporting ppm, a thermocouple reporting a temperature, a pressure transmitter reporting a pressure. The distinction is whether the instrument has an internal threshold or an internal analogue-to-digital conversion across a range.

Dead time and the Smith predictor

Dead time (transport delay) is the enemy of feedback control, because the controller cannot see the effect of its own action until the delay has elapsed. The most common algorithm for compensating dead time is the Smith predictor: it runs an internal model of the process without the delay, predicts what the output would be in the absence of the delay, and feeds that prediction to the controller. The controller then acts on the predicted, undelayed signal rather than on the actual, delayed one.
