1. Fluid Flow

Three relations carry the whole chapter, and every problem uses at least one of them.

Continuity — mass flow is constant along a stream without accumulation:

ṁ = ρ1 A1 v1 = ρ2 A2 v2

Bernoulli — the mechanical energy balance for incompressible, frictionless flow:

P1ρ g + v1²2 g + z1 = P2ρ g + v2²2 g + z2 + hf

Isothermal compressible flow in a long pipe, when the pressure drop is a significant fraction of the inlet pressure:

P1² − P2² = G² R TM [ f LD + 2 ln P1P2 ]

For a gas the density depends on pressure and temperature, so the first step in any gas-flow problem is to compute the density at the section of interest from the ideal-gas law. For a liquid the density is fixed and continuity reduces to Q = A v.

A useful ratio form of continuity for a change of section:

v2v1 = ( D1D2 )²

The velocity ratio is the square of the diameter ratio. Halving the diameter quadruples the velocity.

2. Compression

A compressor raises the pressure of a gas. The theoretical work depends on the path, and three cases bracket the possibilities.

PathWork per unit mass (ideal gas)Character
IsothermalW = (RT/M) ln(P2/P1)Least work; requires perfect cooling
AdiabaticW = (γ/(γ−1))(RT1/M)[(P2/P1)(γ−1)/γ − 1]Most work; no cooling
PolytropicW = (n/(n−1))(RT1/M)[(P2/P1)(n−1)/n − 1]Between the two; partial cooling

The flow form, using the inlet volumetric flow, is more convenient when the problem gives cfm:

W = γγ − 1 P1 V1 [ ( P2P1 )(γ−1)/γ − 1 ]

For multistage compression with perfect intercooling back to the inlet temperature, each stage carries the same pressure ratio and the total work is the number of stages times the work per stage. The optimum pressure ratio per stage is the N-th root of the total ratio:

r = ( PfinalPinitial )1/N

and the pressure after the i-th stage is Pi = Pinitial ri.

The single most useful qualitative fact is that multistage compression with intercooling takes less work than single-stage compression for the same overall pressure ratio. That is the entire point of the comparison questions in this chapter.

Concept discourse: why intercooling saves workCompression heats the gas, and the work of the next stage is proportional to the absolute temperature at which it starts. Cooling back to the inlet temperature before each stage therefore reduces the work of every subsequent stage. The saving is largest when the pressure ratio is large, which is why high-ratio machines are built with several stages and intercoolers. A single-stage machine achieves the same pressure ratio but pays for it in work; a multistage machine pays instead in capital and complexity.

3. Friction

The Darcy–Weisbach equation gives the head lost to friction in a straight pipe:

hf = fD LeqD v²2 g

or, in energy per unit mass,

Ef = fD LeqD v²2

The length Leq is the total equivalent length — the straight pipe plus the equivalent lengths of every fitting and valve. The friction factor is the Darcy factor; the Fanning factor is one quarter of it, and the Fanning form of the equation carries an explicit 4.

For a non-circular duct — an annulus, a rectangular channel — the characteristic length is the hydraulic diameter:

Dh = 4 × flow areawetted perimeter

For an annulus between concentric pipes this reduces to Dh = Douter − Dinner. The flow area is π(Do² − Di²)/4, the velocity is Q/A, and the Reynolds number uses Dh.

For a device that both extracts energy and loses some to friction — a turbine — the energy balance between two points is

P1ρ + v1²2 + g z1 = P2ρ + v2²2 + g z2 + Wshaftη ṁ + Ef

where the shaft work is divided by the efficiency to get the mechanical energy actually removed from the fluid. The friction term is then whatever remains after everything else is accounted for.

4. Metering Devices

Two instruments dominate the chapter, and each has a characteristic correction that is easy to forget.

Pitot tube

The pitot measures the stagnation pressure; a static tap measures the static pressure. Their difference is the dynamic pressure:

ΔP = 12 ρ v² ⇒ v = √2 ΔPρ

When the pitot is connected to a U-tube manometer, the reading is a head of the manometer fluid, and the pressure difference is

ΔP = h ( ρman − ρfluid ) g

For a gas, the fluid density is negligible and ΔP ≈ h ρman g. For a liquid, the fluid density must be subtracted — forgetting that subtraction overstates the pressure difference and therefore the velocity.

Two corrections convert the center reading into a volumetric flow:

  • A pitot coefficient Cp, close to 1.0 for an ideal probe.
  • A center-to-average correction. The center velocity in a turbulent pipe is higher than the average by 15–25 %. For a fully developed turbulent profile, vavg ≈ 0.82 vmax.

Orifice meter

For a sharp-edged orifice in a pipe, the flow is

Q = Cd Ao √2 ΔPρ ( 1 − β⁴ ), β = doD

The discharge coefficient Cd is about 0.6 for a sharp-edged orifice. The (1 − β⁴) factor corrects for the velocity of approach in the pipe upstream; for a small β it is close to 1, but for a large β it matters.

For a mercury manometer, ΔP = h ρHg g, with ρHg = 13 600 kg/m³. The reading h is the differential height of mercury.

Concept discourse: why the manometer fluid density matters for liquids but not for gasesThe U-tube manometer measures a pressure difference by balancing it against a column of manometer fluid. The fluid in the connecting legs also has weight, and that weight partially offsets the column. For a gas, the gas density is a thousandth of the manometer fluid's, so the offset is negligible and ΔP = hρmang is accurate. For a liquid, the offset is a few per cent of the total and cannot be dropped. Using the gas formula on a liquid stream overstates ΔP and therefore overstates the velocity by the square root of the density ratio — about 9 % for water against water, but much more for oil against water. That is the single most common error in pitot problems.

5. Pump

A pump adds energy to a flowing fluid. The extended Bernoulli equation between two points, with the pump head included, is

P1ρ g + v1²2 g + z1 + Hpump = P2ρ g + v2²2 g + z2 + hf

Three simplifications shorten the algebra.

  • Start and end at free surfaces whenever possible. At a free surface open to atmosphere, P = Patm and v ≈ 0, so two of the three terms vanish.
  • Choose the datum at the lower elevation, so one z becomes zero.
  • Group the unknowns on the left: Hpump = (z2 − z1) + hf + (v2² − v1²)/2g + (P2 − P1)/ρg.

The head form and the pressure form are related by the fluid density:

ΔP = ρ g H

so the same head corresponds to a pressure that depends on the fluid. A pump rated at 100 ft of water produces about 54 ft of head of a solution with specific gravity 1.15 at the same pressure.

The three powers that must be distinguished:

PowerFormulaMeaning
HydraulicPhyd = ρ Q g H = ρ Q H (head form)Power delivered to the fluid
Brake (shaft)Pbrake = Phyd / ηPower into the pump shaft
MotorPmotor = Pbrake / ηmotorElectrical power drawn

The head form P = ρQH does not carry a g; the energy form P = ρQgH does. Mixing the two forms is the commonest way to be off by a factor of 9.81 or 32.2.

For a change of speed, the affinity laws apply to a given pump:

Q2Q1 = N2N1, H2H1 = ( N2N1 )², P2P1 = ( N2N1 )³

The exponents 1, 2 and 3 are the point. Power scales with the cube of the speed; using the square instead of the cube is the standard slip.

Worked 5.5: flow rate from a given pressure drop — the iterative case

A pump discharges ethanol at 38 °C into a smooth 50 mm ID tube at 280 kPa gage. The pipe is 40 m long (total equivalent length) and supplies ethanol at 140 kPa gage to a reactor. Negligible elevation change. Density 820 kg/m³, viscosity 0.9 cP. Find the volumetric flow rate.

Show solution
Step 1 — energy-per-mass equation between the two ends; no pump in this segment, no elevation change
P1 − P2ρ = fD LD v²2
Step 2 — left-hand side in J/kg, and the geometric factor L/D
(280 − 140) × 103820 = 170.7 J/kg, 400.05 = 800
Step 3 — the implicit relation in v; the Reynolds number is Re = ρvD/μ = 45 556 v
170.7 = fD × 800 × v²2 ⇒ fD v² = 0.4268
Step 4 — iterate; try v = 5 m/s → Re = 2.28×105, smooth-tube fD ≈ 0.0145 → fDv² = 0.363 (too low)
try v = 5.5 m/s → Re = 2.51×105, fD ≈ 0.0141 → fDv² = 0.427 ✓
Step 5 — flow from the converged velocity
A = π4 ( 0.05 )² = 1.963×10−3 m2
Q = 5.5 × 1.963×10−3 = 1.08×10−2 m3/s ≈ 0.01
The iteration is not optional: v appears on both sides through the friction factor. The answer 0.03 comes from skipping the iteration and assuming a velocity. Two or three passes are always enough, because the friction factor varies only weakly with velocity.

Worked 5.6: discharge rate from a pressure at the pump exit

The pressure after a pump in a 6 in ID smooth pipe carrying water is 20 psia. The water discharges to an open tank 100 ft from the pump. Find the discharge rate in ft³/s.

Show solution
