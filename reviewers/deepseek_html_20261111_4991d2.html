1. Angle Trigonometry

Three families of fact run through this chapter: the geometry of angles (reference angles, coterminal angles, quadrants), the algebra of identities, and the periodicity of the trigonometric functions.

Angles in standard position

An angle in standard position has its vertex at the origin and its initial side along the positive x-axis. The reference angle is the positive acute angle between the terminal side and the x-axis. It is what lets you compute a trigonometric function at any angle from its value at an acute angle.

Quadrant of the terminal sideReference angle
Iθ
II180° − θ
IIIθ − 180°
IV360° − θ

Coterminal angles differ by a whole number of revolutions: θ ± 360°n, or θ ± 2πn. The smallest negative coterminal angle of a positive angle is found by subtracting 360° once.

Periodicity

The period of a trigonometric function is the smallest positive T for which f(t + T) = f(t). For the three main functions in a scaled form:

A sin( Bt + C ): period = 2πB
A cos( Bt + C ): period = 2πB
A tan( Bt + C ): period = πB

Two things are worth fixing in memory. First, the period of tangent is π, not 2π — the tangent function repeats twice as often as sine and cosine. Second, the constant C shifts the graph but does not change its period; only B does.

Identities

The double-angle identities are the workhorses of this chapter, and they are used in both directions — expanding a single angle into a double angle, or collapsing a product into a single function:

sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x
cos 2x = cos²x − sin²x = 1 − 2 sin²x = 2 cos²x − 1
tan 2x = 2 tan x1 − tan²x

The three forms of cos 2x are not three different identities; they are the same identity written three ways using sin²x + cos²x = 1. Choosing the right form is what makes a simplification short.

Two special relations deserve their own lines. The versine is one of the old haversine-family functions, defined by

versin θ = 1 − cos θ

and the conditional tangent identity for a triangle — the sum of the angles equals 180° — is

A + B + C = 180° ⇒ tan A + tan B + tan C = tan A · tan B · tan C

That identity converts a sum of tangents into a product, which is exactly what a question asking for the product wants.

Complementary and inverse functions

The co-function relation connects a trigonometric function to its complement:

sin θ = cos( 90° − θ )

and the inverse functions require a convention. For arcsin, arccos and arctan the principal values are conventionally chosen so the outputs lie in [−90°, 90°] for arcsine and arctangent and [0°, 180°] for arccosine. For arccot two conventions are in circulation: the “principal value” convention with output in (0°, 180°), and the “calculator” convention arccot(x) = arctan(1/x) with output in [−90°, 90°]. The two differ by 180° when x is negative. Read the question's answer options before committing to either.

Worked 1.1: sin 2x from a difference

Given sin x − cos x = −1/3, find the numerical value of sin 2x.

Show solution
Step 1 — square both sides of the given relation
( sin x − cos x )² = ( −1/3 )²
Step 2 — expand the left side using sin²x + cos²x = 1
sin²x − 2 sin x cos x + cos²x = 19
1 − 2 sin x cos x = 19
Step 3 — solve for 2 sin x cos x
2 sin x cos x = 1 − 19 = 89
Step 4 — apply the double-angle identity sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x

The answer 4/9 comes from forgetting the factor of 2 in sin 2x = 2 sin x cos x, or from taking the square root at the end. Squaring the difference is the trick that unlocks the product; the product is sin 2x.

Worked 1.5: versine of a standard angle

What is the numerical value of versin 60°?

Show solution
Step 1 — write the definition of the versine
versin θ = 1 − cos θ
Step 2 — substitute θ = 60° and the standard value cos 60° = 1/2
versin 60° = 1 − 12
Step 3 — evaluate
versin 60° = 1/2
The answer 3/2 comes from using 1 + cos θ instead of 1 − cos θ. The versine is the “versed sine” — the part of the radius that the sine does not cover — and it is small when the angle is small, reaching 2 at 180°. A value of 3/2 at 60° would be larger than the function's value at the very top of the circle, which is impossible.

Worked 1.6: a conditional tangent identity

If A + B + C = 180° and tan A + tan B + tan C = 3, find the numerical value of tan A · tan B · tan C.

Show solution
Step 1 — state the conditional identity for a triangle
A + B + C = 180° ⇒ tan A + tan B + tan C = tan A · tan B · tan C
Step 2 — substitute the given sum
3 = tan A · tan B · tan C
tan A · tan B · tan C = 3
The identity is the entire calculation. Its derivation is worth knowing: from A + B + C = 180°, tan(A + B) = tan(180° − C) = −tan C, and applying the sum formula for tangent to tan(A + B) and clearing fractions gives the product relation.
Variables A, B, C — the three angles of a triangle, summing to 180°
Worked 1.7: a complementary-angle relation

If sin 3A = cos 6B, then what relation holds between A and B?

Show solution
Step 1 — use the co-function identity to express the cosine as a sine
cos 6B = sin( 90° − 6B )
Step 2 — substitute and equate the arguments
sin 3A = sin( 90° − 6B )
3A = 90° − 6B
Step 3 — rearrange into the requested form
3A + 6B = 90°
Step 4 — divide through by 3
A + 2B = 30°
The equality sin u = sin v also allows u = 180° − v, but in this problem the standard interpretation is the direct one, since the question asks for a simple linear relation. The step that makes the problem short is converting the cosine to a sine so the two sides are the same function.
Variables A, B — the angles whose sine and cosine appear
Worked 1.8: reference and coterminal angles

Find the reference angle and the first negative coterminal angle, respectively, of 135°.

Show solution
Step 1 — locate the terminal side: 135° lies in the second quadrant (between 90° and 180°)
quadrant II
Step 2 — the reference angle in quadrant II is 180° minus the angle
reference angle = 180° − 135° = 45°
Step 3 — the smallest negative coterminal angle is found by subtracting one revolution
135° − 360° = −225°

The reference angle is always positive and acute; it is the distance from the terminal side to the nearest part of the x-axis. The negative coterminal angle is the same direction on the circle expressed negatively, and the smallest one is obtained by a single 360° subtraction.

2. Triangle

A triangle has five important centres, four classical congruence relations, and a set of formulas that connect its sides and angles. The questions in this set turn on two of them.

The four classical centres

CentreIntersection ofKey property
Incenterthe angle bisectorsequidistant from all three sides; centre of the inscribed circle
Circumcenterthe perpendicular bisectorsequidistant from all three vertices; centre of the circumscribed circle
Centroidthe mediansdivides each median in the ratio 2:1 from the vertex
Orthocenterthe altitudesthe three altitudes are concurrent

The two that are most easily confused are the incenter and the circumcenter. The mnemonic is in the words: angle bisectors cut the angles and give a circle inside; perpendicular bisectors cut the sides and give a circle around the outside.

Triangle inequality

A set of three lengths forms a triangle if and only if each side is smaller than the sum of the other two:

| a − b | < c < a + b

The left inequality is the one usually forgotten. Two sides of 18 and 6 allow a third side anywhere strictly between 12 and 24; a third side of 11 is too short to close the triangle, no matter how the first two are arranged.

Median and centroid

The median to side a has length

ma = 12 √( 2b² + 2c² − a² )

and the median to the longest side is what a question means by “bisecting the longest side and drawn from the opposite vertex.” The formula needs a, the side being bisected, on its own with the minus sign, and the other two sides squared and doubled with the plus sign. Swapping the role of a with either of the other two is the standard error.

Circumradius, inradius and the distance from the circumcenter to a side

R = a b c4 A, r = As, s = a + b + c2

where A is the area of the triangle, computed by Heron's formula if the three sides are known:

A = √[ s( s − a )( s − b )( s − c ) ]

The perpendicular distance from the circumcenter to a side is found from the right triangle formed by the circumradius, half the side, and that distance:

d = √( R² − ( side2 )² )

The ambiguous case

Two sides and a non-included angle (SSA) do not always determine a unique triangle. The law of sines gives

sin C = c sin Bb

and when the right-hand side lies strictly between 0 and 1 there are two candidate angles for C: the acute one, and its supplement 180° − C. Both give a valid triangle, and both must be checked. The “smallest triangle” is the one with the smaller area, and the area formula

A = 12 b c sin A

(where A is the included angle between sides b and c) then decides which candidate is smaller.

Worked 2.2: the triangle inequality

In triangle ABC, AB = 18 m and BC = 6 m. Which of the following values can be equal to side AC: (A) 6 m, (B) 11 m, (C) 24 m, (D) 13 m?

Show solution
Step 1 — state the triangle inequality with the two known sides
| 18 − 6 | < AC < 18 + 6
Step 2 — simplify
12 < AC < 24
Step 3 — test each option against the strict inequalities
(A) 6 < 12 → not a triangle
(B) 11 < 12 → not a triangle
(C) 24 is not strictly less than 24 → degenerate, not a triangle
(D) 12 < 13 < 24 → valid
AC = 13 m
The lower bound is the one usually missed. A third side of 11 m combined with 18 and 6 is too short: two rods of lengths 18 and 6 placed tip to tip and then bent toward each other cannot close a gap of 11 — the short side plus the 6 m side cannot reach the far end of the 18 m side. The equality cases (AC = 12 or AC = 24) produce a degenerate triangle — the three points lie on a straight line — so the inequalities must be strict.
Variables 18 m, 6 m — the two given sides AC — the third side being tested

3. Circle

A circle inscribed in a triangle touches all three sides; its radius is the inradius r. Two formulas give it, and the choice between them depends on what is known about the triangle.

r = As, s = a + b + c2

where A is the area and s is the semi-perimeter. When the three sides are known and the triangle is not right-angled, the area is computed by Heron's formula first:

A = √[ s( s − a )( s − b )( s − c ) ]

When the triangle is right-angled, the area is half the product of the two legs, and no Heron calculation is needed:

A = 12 ( leg1 )( leg2 )

For a right triangle there is also a one-line formula that bypasses the semi-perimeter entirely:

r = a + b − c2 ( c the hypotenuse )

The circle circumscribed around the triangle is a different circle, with radius R = abc/(4A). A question that asks for the circle “inscribed in” the triangle wants r, and a question that asks for the circle “circumscribing” the triangle wants R. The numbers are close for a nearly equilateral triangle and wildly different for a long thin one, so the wording matters.

4. Trigonometric Leveling

Trigonometric leveling computes heights from measured angles and horizontal distances. Every problem in this chapter reduces to the same three moves: draw the vertical right triangle, identify the angle given (elevation, depression, or subtended), and write the tangent relation.

Angles of elevation and depression

The angle of elevation is measured upward from the horizontal; the angle of depression is measured downward from the horizontal. Because the horizontal lines at the observer and at the target are parallel, the angle of depression from A to B equals the angle of elevation from B to A — a fact that lets a downward measurement be converted into an upward one.

tan θ = oppositeadjacent = heighthorizontal distance

Two-angle problems

The standard “angle of elevation is α at a point and β nearer” problem is solved by writing the height twice and equating:

h = x tan β = ( x + d ) tan α

where x is the distance from the nearer point and d is the distance between the two observation points. Solving for x gives x = d tan α/(tan β − tan α), and h = x tan β. The two equations are the whole method; the algebra is one substitution.

Angles subtended by two stacked objects

When two stacked objects — a flagpole on a pedestal, for example — subtend equal angles from a point on the ground, the condition is an equality of angle differences:

arctan( hlowerx ) = arctan( hupperx ) − arctan( hlowerx )

Rearranged, that is 2 arctan(hlower/x) = arctan(hupper/x), which is solved with the double-angle formula for tangent.

A leaned pole

When the pole is not vertical, the height and the horizontal offset of its top are no longer (L, 0) but (L cos φ, L sin φ), where φ is the lean from the vertical. The tangent relation becomes

tan θ = L cos φd + L sin φ

with θ the sun's elevation and d the shadow length. The two terms in the denominator are what make the lean problem harder than the vertical one: the shadow is longer by L sin φ, and the height is shorter by (1 − cos φ).

Angles of depression between two buildings

When an observer high on one building looks down at the top and the foot of another, the two angles of depression both open from the observer's horizontal line. The vertical drop to the top of the lower building is d tan(top angle) and the vertical drop to the ground is d tan(foot angle). The height of the lower building is the difference:

hlower = d ( tan θfoot − tan θtop )

and the horizontal distance d is fixed by the observer's own height above ground:

d = Hobservertan θfoot

Remember to add the observer's eye height to the height of the building he stands on before doing the calculation.
