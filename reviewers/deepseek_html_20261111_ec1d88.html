1. Integral Equations (Techniques)

Every integral in this chapter falls into one of six families. Naming the family before touching the integrand is what keeps the work short.

Substitution

∫ f( g(x) ) g′(x) dx = ∫ f(u) du

The u must swallow the entire inner function and its derivative must be present (up to a constant). When the derivative is present only partially — say a leftover factor of x when u involves x² — the substitution is not complete and the integral will not close.

Integration by parts

∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du

The LIATE order — Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential — picks u: whichever function appears earlier in that list is the u. For x³cos x the algebraic x³ comes before the trig cos x, so u = x³. For e2xsin 3x both are in the ambiguous middle; either choice works and the process is two rounds of parts, after which the integral reappears on the right and can be solved algebraically.

Partial fractions

A proper rational P/Q is decomposed by the factorization of Q. For distinct linear factors, one term per factor; for a repeated factor (z − a)ⁿ, the powers (z − a)¹ through (z − a)ⁿ each get a term; for an irreducible quadratic, the numerator is a linear term Bz + C. The unknown coefficients are found by clearing denominators and substituting convenient values of the variable (the roots of the linear factors) or by matching coefficients.

√( a² + x² ) : x = a tan θ, dx = a sec² θ dθ

2. Angle Trigonometry

Trigonometric integrals of the form ∫ sinmx cosnx dx over [0, π/2] have a closed form that avoids any integration at all. The Wallis formula:

∫0π/2 sinmx cosnx dx = (m−1)!! (n−1)!!(m+n)!! (both m, n odd)
= (m−1)!! (n−1)!!(m+n)!! · π2 (both m, n even)

Here (2k)!! = 2·4·6…(2k) and (2k+1)!! = 1·3·5…(2k+1). The double factorial notation is worth practising: 20!! = 2^10·10!, 32!! = 2^16·16!, 54!! = 2^27·27!.

When both m and n are odd

No π/2 factor appears; the answer is the ratio of double factorials directly. This is the case most likely to appear in the exam, since the answer is a small fraction.

When at least one is even

The π/2 factor appears; if both are even, it appears; if only one is even, the formula is the product form divided by (m+n)!! and multiplied by π/2.

Products of unlike trig functions

Products of sin ax · cos bx are handled with the product-to-sum identities first, then each resulting term integrates to a simple sine or cosine:

sin A cos B = ½[ sin(A + B) + sin(A − B) ]
sin A sin B = ½[ cos(A − B) − cos(A + B) ]
cos A cos B = ½[ cos(A − B) + cos(A + B) ]

2. Angle Trigonometry (Wallis Integrals)

Trigonometric integrals of the form ∫ sinmx cosnx dx over the interval [0, π/2] have a closed form that avoids integration entirely. The Wallis formula:

∫0π/2 sinmx cosnx dx = (m−1)!! (n−1)!!(m+n)!! (both m, n odd)
= (m−1)!! (n−1)!!(m+n)!! · π2 (both m, n even)

Here (2k)!! = 2·4·6…(2k) = 2k·k!, and (2k+1)!! = 1·3·5…(2k+1). For mixed parity, the factor of π/2 appears once. The formula is exact for the definite integral on [0, π/2] and does not apply outside that interval.

Products of unlike trig functions

When the integrand is a product of sin ax and cos bx with different frequencies, the product-to-sum identities reduce it to a sum of simple sines and cosines:

sin A cos B = ½[ sin(A + B) + sin(A − B) ]
sin A sin B = ½[ cos(A − B) − cos(A + B) ]
cos A cos B = ½[ cos(A − B) + cos(A + B) ]

3. Circle (Arc Length)

The arc length of a curve y = f(x) between x = a and x = b is

L = ∫ab √( 1 + ( y′ )² ) dx

For a curve given parametrically x = x(t), y = y(t) between t1 and t2:

L = ∫t1t2 √( ( dx/dt )² + ( dy/dt )² ) dt

Circle of radius R

For x² + y² = R², taking the upper branch y = √(R² − x²):

y′ = −x√( R² − x² )
√( 1 + ( y′ )² ) = R√( R² − x² )

so the arc length is

L = R ∫ab dx√( R² − x² ) = R [ arcsin( x / R ) ]ab

The formula is the geometric fact that arc length equals radius times the angle subtended (in radians), and the arcsin is exactly that angle.

Verification shortcut

The full circumference of a circle is 2πR; half is πR; a quarter is πR/2. Any partial arc can be checked against these anchors: if the arc from x = 0 to x = R comes out as πR/2, the setup is right; if it comes out as something else, one of the limits or the integrand was mis-set.

4. Area

Three area formulas cover the chapter. Which one to use depends on how the boundary is described.

Area between two Cartesian curves

A = ∫ab [ f(x) − g(x) ] dx

with f above g on the interval. If the curves cross inside the interval, split at the crossing points and integrate each piece separately.

Area in polar coordinates

A = ½ ∫αβ r² dθ

For a region bounded by two polar curves, use the difference of squares: A = ½∫(router² − rinner²)dθ, with the angular limits at the intersection angles.

Surface of revolution

S = 2π ∫ab y √( 1 + ( y′ )² ) dx (about the x-axis)

The arc-length element √(1 + (y′)²) is the part most often dropped; leaving it out gives only the area of a stack of cylinders, not the true tilted surface. For a paraboloid x² = 4py the surface is

S = 2π ∫0r x √( 1 + ( x / 2p )² ) dx

which evaluates to the closed form

S = 8πp²3 [ ( 1 + ( r/2p )² )3/2 − 1 ]

5. Centroid

The centroid (center of area) of a plane region is the point (x̄, ȳ) where the region would balance. Its coordinates are the first moments divided by the area:

A = ∫ab [ f(x) − g(x) ] dx
x̄ = 1A ∫ab x [ f(x) − g(x) ] dx
ȳ = 1A ∫ab ½ [ f(x)² − g(x)² ] dx

The factor ½ in the y-moment comes from the fact that the strip at height x has its own centroid at half-height.

Reading the integrals correctly

  • The x̄ integrand is x times the height: a moment arm times an area element.
  • The ȳ integrand is half the difference of squares: because the strip's height is [f − g], the moment of that strip about the x-axis is [f − g]·(f + g)/2.
  • Both integrals use the same limits as the area integral.

6. Moment

The moment of inertia of a plane region about an axis is the integral of the squared distance from that axis, weighted by the area element:

Iy = ∫∫ x² dA (about the y-axis)
Ix = ∫∫ y² dA (about the x-axis)

The squared distance is what distinguishes a moment from a first moment (centroid): a first moment weights by x, a second moment by x². That is why a region far from the axis can have a huge I even if its area is modest.

The strip formulas

For a region described vertically (x from a to b, y from g(x) to f(x)):

Iy = ∫ab x² [ f(x) − g(x) ] dx

For a region described horizontally (y from c to d, x from g(y) to f(y)):

Iy = ∫cd [ f(y)³ − g(y)³ ]3 dy

Choose the description that gives the simpler integrand.

Sign and unit checks

I is never negative. It has units of length to the fourth power, so a length is always inside a squared factor. And Iy of a region that hugs the y-axis is small; Iy of a region far to the right is large.

7. Integral Volume

Volumes of revolution have three standard methods. The choice depends on the axis of revolution and on how the boundary is described.

Disk and washer (perpendicular slices)

disk about x-axis: V = π ∫ab y² dx
washer about x-axis: V = π ∫ab ( R² − r² ) dx

with R the outer radius and r the inner radius at height x.

Cylindrical shells (parallel strips)

shells about y-axis: V = 2π ∫ab x h(x) dx

with h(x) the height of the region at distance x from the axis. Use shells when the region is described more simply as y = f(x) than as x = g(y).

Pappus's theorems

First theorem (area of a surface of revolution):

S = 2π · ( arc length ) · ( distance from axis to arc centroid )

Second theorem (volume of a solid of revolution):

V = 2π · A · d

with A the area of the region and d the distance from the axis to the region's centroid. Pappus is the fastest route for an ellipse, a triangle, or any region whose centroid and area are already known.

Work to pump a liquid

W = ρ g ∫ ( depth below the outlet ) · A(z) dz

The integral sums the work to lift each horizontal slice from its depth to the outlet.
