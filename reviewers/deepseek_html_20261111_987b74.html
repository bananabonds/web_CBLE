1. Electrostatics

Three laws cover the chapter: Gauss's law for the electric flux, the Biot–Savart law for the magnetic field of a current, and Faraday's law for the induced emf.

Gauss's law

The electric flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by the permittivity of free space:

ΦE = Qenclosedε₀, ε₀ = 8.85×10−12 C²/(N·m²)

Only the enclosed charge matters; the shape of the Gaussian surface does not. For an electron gas of N electrons, Qenclosed = −N e (with e = 1.6×10−19 C), and the flux magnitude is Ne/ε₀.

Magnetic field of a long straight wire

B = μ₀ I2 π r, μ₀ = 4π×10−7 T·m/A

The field falls off as 1/r. A distance of a few centimetres from a few amps produces a field of a few microteslas — about the strength of the Earth's magnetic field.

Faraday's law

An electromotive force is induced in a loop whenever the magnetic flux through the loop changes:

ε = −N dΦBdt, ΦB = B A cos θ

The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced emf drives a current whose own magnetic field opposes the change. For a single loop (N = 1) with the field perpendicular to the loop area (cos θ = 1):

|ε| = A |ΔB|Δt

Two disciplines are essential. The area must be in square metres, not square centimetres. And the rate is ΔB/Δt, not B itself; if the field is reduced by 0.08 T in 0.02 s, the rate is 4 T/s, not 0.08 T.

Worked 1.1: induced emf in a loop

A loop of area 200 cm² is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field. Without changing the field's direction, its magnitude is reduced by 0.08 T in 0.02 s. Find the average induced emf.

Show solution
Step 1 — convert the area to square metres
A = 200 cm² = 200 × 10−4 m² = 0.020 m²
Step 2 — apply Faraday's law for a single loop, perpendicular field
|ε| = A ΔBΔt
Step 3 — substitute
|ε| = 0.020 × 0.080.02 = 0.020 × 4
Step 4 — evaluate
|ε| = 0.08 V
The answer 0.06 V comes from using the change in field (0.08 T) directly instead of the rate of change (4 T/s). Faraday's law is about the rate: the same ΔB over a longer Δt gives a smaller emf. Writing ΔB/Δt on its own line before multiplying by A is the safeguard.
Variables A — loop area, 0.020 m² ΔB — change in magnetic field, 0.08 T Δt — time interval, 0.02 s ε — induced emf, V

2. Minimum/Maximum (Energy Conservation)

For a body moving under gravity on a frictionless track, the total mechanical energy is constant:

E = 12 m v² + m g h = constant

The mass cancels in every comparison, so the calculation is purely about v²/(2g) versus h:

hmax = hstart + vstart²2 g

The term v²/(2g) is the kinetic head: how much additional height the body could reach if all its kinetic energy were converted to potential energy. It is the reason a roller coaster car passing a low point fast can climb a slightly higher hill than the one it started from.

Direction of the comparison

  • If the cart starts at rest (v = 0), it can reach exactly the height it started from, and no higher.
  • If the cart starts moving, the kinetic head adds to the starting height and the cart can climb slightly higher.
  • If the question asks for "maximum height before it stops," the answer is the starting height plus the kinetic head — a stop means v = 0, so all kinetic energy has become potential.
Worked 2.1: maximum height on a frictionless track

A cart travels along a frictionless roller coaster track. At point A, the cart is 10 m above the ground and traveling at 2 m/s. What is the maximum height the cart can reach before it stops?

Show solution
Step 1 — energy conservation between point A and the maximum-height point
12 m vA² + m g hA = 12 m vmax² + m g hmax
Step 2 — the cart "stops" at maximum height, so vmax = 0; the mass cancels
hmax = hA + vA²2 g
Step 3 — compute the kinetic head
vA²2 g = ( 2 )²2 ( 9.8 ) = 419.6 = 0.204 m
Step 4 — add to the starting height
hmax = 10 + 0.204 = 10.204 m ≈ 10.2 m
The answer 11.3 m comes from a slip in the kinetic head — often using v/g instead of v²/(2g), which gives 0.204 → 2×0.204 = 0.408 and would give 10.4, not 11.3; or from an arithmetic slip. The kinetic head is 20 cm here, only 2 % of the starting height, which is why the answer is so close to 10.
Variables hA — starting height, 10 m vA — starting speed, 2 m/s g — gravitational acceleration, 9.8 m/s² hmax — maximum height reached, m

3. Mirror Equation and Refraction

Three formulas cover this chapter: the mirror equation, the thin-lens equation, and Snell's law of refraction. Each comes with a sign convention that must be stated before substituting.

Mirror equation

1f = 1v + 1u, m = −vu, R = 2 f

With the "real is positive" convention:

QuantitySign
Object distance upositive for a real object in front of the mirror
Image distance vpositive for a real image (in front), negative for virtual
Focal length fpositive for concave, negative for convex
Magnification mnegative for inverted, positive for erect

What each mirror does

MirrorImage of a real object
Convex (f < 0)always virtual, erect, smaller than the object
Concave, object beyond freal, inverted
Concave, object at fat infinity
Concave, object inside fvirtual, erect, larger (the shaving-mirror case)

Thin lens and lensmaker's equation

1f = 1v − 1u (lens sign convention)
1f = ( n − 1 ) ( 1R₁ + 1R₂ ) (lensmaker, positive-convex convention)

The lensmaker form above uses R positive for a convex surface (as seen from outside the lens) and negative for a concave one. A biconvex lens has both R positive and is converging; a biconcave has both negative and is diverging; a meniscus has opposite signs and is converging when the convex surface is stronger (smaller radius).

Refraction and apparent depth

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ (Snell)
apparent depth = real depthn (viewing from air into a medium of index n)

An object at the bottom of a pool looks shallower than it is; the factor is exactly the refractive index. The reciprocal relation gives the real depth from the apparent depth.

Worked 3.1: properties of a convex mirror image

The image produced by a convex mirror of an erect object in front of the mirror is always ______.

Show solution
Step 1 — the focal length of a convex mirror is negative (f < 0)
Step 2 — apply the mirror equation with u positive; 1/v = 1/f − 1/u
1v = 1f − 1u = negative − positive = negative
v < 0 ⇒ virtual image
Step 3 — magnification m = −v/u; v negative, u positive, so m = positive
m > 0 ⇒ erect image
Step 4 — magnitude of m is less than 1 because |v| < u for a convex mirror
|m| < 1 ⇒ smaller than the object
virtual, erect, smaller than the object
The answer "real, erect, larger" combines an impossible sign pattern: a real image from a convex mirror of a real object cannot occur (the reflected rays diverge, never converge). The sign algebra settles it in one line: f negative forces v negative, and v negative forces m positive. Every property is fixed by the sign of f.
Variables f — focal length, negative for convex u — object distance, positive v — image distance, negative for a virtual image m — magnification
Worked 3.2: concave mirror, real image twice the object size

A concave mirror forms a real image twice the size of the object. If the object is 20 cm from the mirror, the radius of curvature must be about ______.

Show solution
Step 1 — set up the signs: u = +20 cm (real object), a real image has v > 0, and a twice-size real image from a concave mirror is inverted, so m = −2
u = 20 cm, m = −2
Step 2 — apply m = −v/u to find v
−2 = −v20 ⇒ v = 40 cm
Step 3 — apply the mirror equation
1f = 120 + 140 = 340
f = 403 = 13.33 cm
Step 4 — the radius of curvature is twice the focal length
R = 2 f = 803 = 26.7 ≈ 27 cm
The answer 13 cm is the focal length, not the radius. The factor of 2 between f and R is the trap; every mirror problem ends with R = 2f as a separate step, not as a shortcut. For a lens, no such factor exists — the lens equation uses f directly.
Variables u — object distance, 20 cm v — image distance, 40 cm f — focal length, 13.33 cm R — radius of curvature, 26.7 cm

Worked 3.5: meniscus lens from the lensmaker's equation

A lens has a convex surface of radius 17 cm and a concave surface of radius 38 cm and is made of glass of refractive index 1.55. Find its focal length and classify it as converging or diverging.

Show solution
Step 1 — sign convention: R positive for a surface that bulges outward, negative for one that curves inward
R₁ = +17 cm (convex), R₂ = −38 cm (concave)
Step 2 — apply the lensmaker's equation
1f = ( n − 1 )( 1R₁ + 1R₂ )
Step 3 — substitute n = 1.55
1f = 0.55 × ( 117 − 138 )
Step 4 — evaluate the bracket
117 = 0.05882, 138 = 0.02632
bracket = 0.05882 − 0.02632 = 0.03250
Step 5 — multiply by (n − 1)
1f = 0.55 × 0.03250 = 0.017875 cm−1
Step 6 — invert
f = 10.017875 = 55.9 cm
Step 7 — positive focal length means converging
converging, f ≈ 55.9 cm
The answer 57.45 cm comes from a slip in the bracket — often from using the magnitudes 1/17 + 1/38 = 0.0851 instead of their difference. The lens is converging because the convex surface (smaller radius, stronger curve) dominates the concave surface. If the concave surface were the stronger one (smaller radius), the lens would be diverging; the sign of f follows the sign of the bracket.
Variables R₁ — radius of convex surface, +17 cm R₂ — radius of concave surface, −38 cm n — refractive index, 1.55 f — focal length, 55.9 cm
Worked 3.6: apparent depth in amber

An insect preserved in amber (n ≈ 1.54) appears to be 2.78 cm beneath the surface when viewed directly from above. Find its real depth.

Show solution
Step 1 — the apparent-depth relation for viewing from air into a medium of index n
apparent depth = real depthn
Step 2 — solve for real depth
real depth = n × apparent depth
Step 3 — substitute
real depth = 1.54 × 2.78
Step 4 — evaluate
real depth = 4.28 cm ≈ 4.29 cm
The answer 3.19 cm comes from dividing by n instead of multiplying. The apparent depth is always less than the real depth (the object looks shallower than it is), so the real depth must be larger than 2.78; dividing would give 2.78/1.54 = 1.81, which is even smaller, and any answer below 2.78 is immediately wrong. The direction of the correction is a quick self-check.
Variables n — refractive index of amber, 1.54 apparent depth — 2.78 cm real depth — the physical depth of the insect, cm
Worked 3.7: converging lens image size

An object is 30 cm in front of a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. The image is ______.

Show solution
Step 1 — object distance u = 30 cm, focal length f = +10 cm
Step 2 — the lens equation with "real is positive" convention
1f = 1v − 1u
Step 3 — solve for v
1v = 110 + 130 = 3 + 130 = 430
v = 304 = 7.5 cm
Step 4 — positive v means real; the magnification is m = −v/u
m = −7.530 = −0.25
|m| = 0.25 < 1 ⇒ smaller than the object
real and smaller than the object
The answer "real and larger" would require the object to be between f and 2f, not beyond 2f. Here u = 30 = 3f, so the object is well outside 2f = 20, and the image is smaller and between f and 2f on the other side. The rule of thumb: beyond 2f gives a smaller real image; between f and 2f gives a larger real image; inside f gives a virtual image.

4. Waves

Sound in a tube is described by standing waves whose boundary conditions depend on whether the ends are open or closed. The speed of sound in a gas depends on temperature and molar mass; in a solid it depends on the elastic modulus and density; and a moving source or observer shifts the observed frequency.

Standing waves in pipes

BoundaryResonant frequenciesHarmonics
Both ends openfn = n v / (2 L)all integers (n = 1, 2, 3, …)
One end closedfn = n v / (4 L)odd integers only (n = 1, 3, 5, …)

The "5th harmonic present" for a closed pipe is not n = 5; the closed pipe skips even harmonics, so the harmonic sequence is 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, … and the 5th one present is n = 9. Reading "5th harmonic" as "n = 5" is the standard error.

Speed of sound in a gas

v = √γ R TM

with γ = Cp/Cv (≈1.4 for diatomic air), R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), T in kelvin, and M in kg/mol.

Speed of sound in a solid

v = √Yρ

with Y the Young's modulus (Pa) and ρ the density (kg/m³). The result for steel is typically in the thousands of metres per second.

Doppler effect

f′ = f v ± vobsv ∓ vsrc

The signs are chosen by the rule that the frequency rises when source and observer approach and falls when they recede:

MotionNumerator (observer)Denominator (source)
Observer moves toward sourcev + vobs—
Observer moves away from sourcev − vobs—
Source moves toward observer—v − vsrc
Source moves away from observer—v + vsrc

When both are moving, the numerator carries the observer's sign and the denominator carries the source's sign independently. Approach increases f′ (numerator up, denominator down); recession decreases f′ (numerator down, denominator up).

Show solution

Step 2 — the 5th harmonic present corresponds to n = 9
n = 9
Step 3 — apply the closed-pipe resonance formula

The answer 276 Hz is what comes from using n = 5 (the integer "5th harmonic") — but closed pipes have no even harmonics, so the fifth one present is n = 9. Counting the harmonics that exist (1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th) rather than the n value is the whole point of the wording. The 1st is n=1, the 2nd is n=3, the 3rd is n=5, the 4th is n=7, the 5th is n=9.
Variables L — pipe length, 2.29 m v — speed of sound, 343 m/s n — harmonic number, 9 (odd)

A steel rod has Young's modulus Y = 2×1011 Pa and density ρ = 8 000 kg/m³. Find the speed of sound in the steel.

Show solution

Worked 4.5: Doppler — approaching

A police car moves toward an observer at 25 m/s, and the observer moves toward the car at 5 m/s. Siren frequency 600 Hz. Find the observed frequency. Speed of sound 350 m/s.

Show solution
Step 1 — both observer and source are moving toward each other, so frequency rises
Step 2 — apply the Doppler formula: numerator v + vobs, denominator v − vsrc
f′ = f v + vobsv − vsrc
Step 3 — substitute
f′ = 600 × 350 + 5350 − 25 = 600 × 355325
Step 4 — evaluate the ratio
ratio = 1.0923
Step 5 — multiply
f′ = 600 × 1.0923 = 655.4 ≈ 655 Hz
The answer 630 Hz comes from using the same sign in numerator and denominator (355/350 = 1.014 × 600 = 608, not 630) — or from the wrong pairing of signs. Both approach signs must be applied: observer's speed in the numerator with a plus, source's speed in the denominator with a minus. Getting one of them wrong gives 635 or 608; only both correct gives 655.
Variables f — source frequency, 600 Hz v — speed of sound, 350 m/s vobs — observer's speed toward source, 5 m/s vsrc — source's speed toward observer, 25 m/s
Worked 4.6: Doppler — receding

A police car moves away from an observer at 25 m/s, and the observer moves away from the car at 5 m/s. Siren frequency 600 Hz. Find the observed frequency. Speed of sound 350 m/s.

Show solution
Step 1 — both are moving away, so frequency falls
Step 2 — apply the Doppler formula: numerator v − vobs, denominator v + vsrc
f′ = f v − vobsv + vsrc
Step 3 — substitute
f′ = 600 × 350 − 5350 + 25 = 600 × 345375
Step 4 — evaluate the ratio
ratio = 0.92
Step 5 — multiply
f′ = 600 × 0.92 = 552 Hz
The answer 552 Hz is below 600 Hz, which is correct: recession lowers the observed frequency. The mirror-image structure of the two Doppler problems (655 for approach, 552 for recession) means the two answers straddle the source frequency symmetrically in ratio, not in Hz. Checking the direction of the shift against the direction of motion catches a swapped sign in one line.
Variables f — source frequency, 600 Hz v — speed of sound, 350 m/s vobs — observer's speed away from source, 5 m/s vsrc — source's speed away from observer, 25 m/s

Mirror — lunar image radius of curvature (key: 2.2 m)Verified: |m| = 0.010/3.476×106 = 2.877×10−9; v = |m| × 3.84×108 = 1.105 m; f ≈ v ≈ 1.105 m; R ≈ 2.21 m. The 2.2 cm distractor is a unit slip (cm instead of m).
Mirror — object inside focal length (key: erect and virtual)Verified: u = 4, f = 6, 1/v = 1/6 − 1/4 = −1/12, v = −12 cm (virtual), m = +3 (erect). The object is inside f, so the concave mirror behaves as a magnifying glass.
Mirror — meniscus lens (key: 55.93 cm, converging)Verified: 1/f = (1.55−1)(1/17 − 1/38) = 0.55 × 0.0325 = 0.017875; f = 55.9 cm. Positive focal length means converging; the convex surface (smaller radius) dominates.
Mirror — apparent depth in amber (key: 4.29 cm)Verified: real = n × apparent = 1.54 × 2.78 = 4.28 cm. The 3.19 cm distractor is roughly n × 2.07 (or a similar slip); the direction check — real depth must exceed 2.78 cm — rules it out immediately.
