1. Compression

A compressor raises the pressure of a gas by doing work on it. The work depends on the path: isothermal, adiabatic, or polytropic. The governing relations for an ideal gas are:

isothermal: W = P1 V1 ln P2P1 = R T ln P2P1
adiabatic: T2 = T1 ( P2P1 )(γ−1)/γ, W = cp ( T2 − T1 )
polytropic PVn: T2 = T1 ( P2P1 )(n−1)/n, W = R ( T2 − T1 )1 − n

The three paths bracket the possibilities: isothermal compression requires the least work, adiabatic the most, and polytropic lies between. The exponent n is read from the process conditions (n = 1 isothermal, n = γ adiabatic, n between 1 and γ for a cooled machine).

2. Entropy

Entropy is a state function. For an ideal gas the change between two states is:

ΔS = cp ln T2T1 − R ln P2P1

For a phase change at constant temperature, ΔS = ΔH/T. For a heating path, ΔS = ∫(cp/T)dT. The entropy balance for a steady-flow device is

Ṡgen = ṁ Δs − QT

and the combined first-and-second-law energy balance gives the work directly when Q and ΔS are both known:

W = Q − ΔH (steady flow)

Variables Tb — boiling point at the operating pressure, 398 K (125 °C) cp,l, cp,g — liquid and gas heat capacities, J/mol·K ΔHvap — enthalpy of vaporisation at the boiling point, J/mol ΔS — total entropy change, 75 J/mol·K
Concept discourse: entropy is a state function, so choose the pathThe total ΔS between two states is independent of the actual path; only the endpoints matter. When the real path is unknown (as in a hot-environment compression), substitute a convenient reversible path between the same endpoints. That is what makes the ideal-gas formula usable: the given initial and final states are connected by a reversible heating-then-expanding path, and the entropy difference comes out the same. The same logic drives the boiling-point calculation: the ΔS is split into sensible heating on each side of the phase change because each piece has its own formula.

3. Equations of State

The ideal-gas law PV = RT is the baseline. Real gases deviate, and the deviations are captured by:

  • Compressibility factor Z: PV = ZRT. Z → 1 at low P and high T.
  • Virial equation: Z = 1 + B/V + C/V² + … or Z = 1 + BP/RT + (C−B²)(P/RT)² + …
  • van der Waals: P = RT/(V−b) − a/V².
  • Generalized correlations: Z = Z⁰(Tr, Pr) + ω Z¹(Tr, Pr), with Tr = T/Tc and Pr = P/Pc.

The choice depends on the conditions. At low pressure the truncated virial (up to B) is exact; at high pressure the generalized Z chart or a cubic equation of state is needed. The van der Waals equation is useful for illustrating the pressure–volume correction and for computing work along a reversible path, but it is not accurate for quantitative design.

4. Internal Energy

The first law in its simplest form for a closed system is:

ΔU = Q − W

with W defined as work done by the system. Every other convention (W done on the system, enthalpy instead of internal energy) follows from rearranging this statement. The signs are the whole content of the question:

  • Heat into the system: Q > 0. Heat out: Q < 0.
  • Work done by the system: W > 0. Work done on the system: W < 0.
  • Internal energy rises when the left side is positive, and falls when the left side is negative.
Worked 4.1: sign of the internal-energy change

During the working stroke of an engine, 150 kJ/kg of heat is transferred out of the system and the engine does 250 kJ/kg of work. Find the change in internal energy.

Show solution
Step 1 — assign signs under the convention ΔU = Q − Wby
Q = −150 kJ/kg (heat out)
Wby = +250 kJ/kg (work done by the engine)
Step 2 — apply the first law
ΔU = −150 − 250 = −400 kJ/kg
The answer +400 comes from flipping one sign — either treating the heat loss as positive (Q = +150) or treating the work as done on the system (W = −250 with Q positive). Both errors produce +400. The system loses energy both ways: heat leaves and work is done, so the internal energy must fall.
Variables Q — heat transferred to the system, kJ/kg Wby — work done by the system, kJ/kg ΔU — change in specific internal energy, kJ/kg
Concept discourse: one convention, consistently appliedTextbooks differ on whether W means work done by or on the system. The physics is identical; only the bookkeeping is different. Pick one convention at the start of a problem and write the sign of every term explicitly — “heat leaves, so Q = −150”, “work done by, so W = +250” — and the first-law calculation follows mechanically. The wrong answer usually comes from mixing the two conventions within one line, which produces the correct magnitude with the wrong sign — exactly the pattern in this question.

5. Nozzles

A nozzle converts enthalpy into kinetic energy. For a steady-flow adiabatic nozzle with negligible inlet velocity, the energy balance is

h1 + V1²2 = h2 + V2²2

so the exit velocity is

V2 = √[ 2 ( h1 − h2 ) + V1² ]

The units matter: h in kJ/kg means the bracket must be multiplied by 1000 to bring it into m²/s², because 1 kJ/kg = 1000 m²/s². Forgetting that factor of 1000 is the commonest error in a nozzle problem.

The exit area (and therefore the exit diameter) follows from continuity:

A1 V1 / v1 = A2 V2 / v2 = ṁ

with v the specific volume.

6. Phase Equilibria

Two phases coexist at a given temperature and pressure only if their chemical potentials are equal. The Clapeyron equation expresses that condition as a relation between the slope of the coexistence line and the changes in enthalpy and volume:

dPdT = ΔHT ΔV

Two special cases follow. For vaporisation (ideal gas, Vg >> Vl) the Clapeyron equation integrates to the Clausius–Clapeyron form:

ln P2P1 = ΔHvapR ( 1T1 − 1T2 )

For solid–liquid equilibrium (the ice-skating problem) the equation is used directly in the differential form, with ΔV = Vl − Vs typically small and negative for water.

For an approximate calculation over a small temperature range, the linearised form is enough:

ΔP = ΔHT ΔV ΔT

7. Steam Tables

Steam tables give the thermodynamic properties of water as functions of two independent properties — usually P and T, or P and x (quality). Three states must be distinguished.

StateConditionHow to look up
Compressed (subcooled) liquidT < Tsat(P) or P > Psat(T)Compressed-liquid table, or approximate as saturated liquid at T
Saturated mixturevf < v < vg or sf < s < sgQuality x from the given property; other properties by interpolation
Superheated vapourT > Tsat(P)Superheated table at the given P and T

The quality is the fraction by mass of the mixture that is vapour:

x = v − vfvg − vf = s − sfsg − sf

and any mixture property follows from the linear interpolation:

h = hf + x hfg, s = sf + x sfg

The same relations apply for the enthalpy in a turbine or condenser: find the inlet state from the tables, find the outlet state from the quality, subtract.

8. Thermodynamic Cycles

A Rankine cycle is the ideal cycle for a steam power plant. It has four processes:

  • Pump: liquid water compressed from condenser pressure to boiler pressure. Work ≈ vf ΔP, usually small compared with turbine work.
  • Boiler: isobaric heating and vaporisation, then superheat.
  • Turbine: isentropic or actual expansion, extracting work.
  • Condenser: isobaric condensation to saturated liquid.

The thermal efficiency is

η = WnetQin = ( h1 − h2 ) − wpumph1 − h4

with state 1 at turbine inlet, 2 at turbine outlet, 3 at condenser outlet, and 4 at pump outlet. The pump work is usually 1–3 % of the turbine work and is sometimes neglected in preliminary calculations.

For the turbine itself, the actual work differs from the isentropic work by the turbine efficiency:

wturb,actual = ηt ( h1 − h2s )
Worked 8.1: Rankine cycle power and efficiency

9. Thermodynamic Processes

The first law applies to any process. What changes between problems is the work expression, which depends on the path:

isobaric: W = P ( V2 − V1 ) = R ( T2 − T1 )
isochoric: W = 0
isothermal: W = R T ln V2V1 = R T ln P1P2
adiabatic: W = R ( T1 − T2 )γ − 1 = Cv ( T1 − T2 )
polytropic: W = R ( T1 − T2 )n − 1

The heat follows from the energy balance once the work and the internal-energy change are known. For an ideal gas with constant specific heats, ΔU = Cv (T2 − T1) always, whatever the path.

When the specific heat varies with temperature, the heat capacity integral replaces the simple product:

ΔH = ∫T1T2 Cp (T) dT

and the isentropic relation becomes an integral that usually needs iteration:

∫T1T2 Cp (T)T dT = R ln P2P1
Worked 9.1: isentropic expansion of an ideal gas with variable Cp

Worked 9.3: steady-flow compressor work

Air flows steadily at 0.5 kg/s through a compressor. Inlet: 7 m/s, 100 kPa, 0.95 m³/kg. Outlet: 5 m/s, 700 kPa, 0.19 m³/kg. Internal energy of the air leaving is 90 kJ/kg greater than at inlet. Cooling water removes 58 kW. Find the work in kW.

Show solution
Step 1 — steady-flow energy balance per unit mass
q − w = Δh + Δke + Δpe
Step 2 — Δh from Δu and Δ(Pv)
Δh = Δu + ( P2 v2 − P1 v1 ) = 90 + ( 700×0.19 − 100×0.95 )
Δh = 90 + ( 133 − 95 ) = 128 kJ/kg
Step 3 — Δke, in kJ/kg
Δke = ( 5² − 7² ) / 2 000 = −0.012 kJ/kg
Step 4 — heat per unit mass (negative: heat removed)
q = −580.5 = −116 kJ/kg
Step 5 — solve for work
w = q − Δh − Δke = −116 − 128 + 0.012 = −243.99 kJ/kg
W = w ṁ = −243.99 × 0.5 = −122 kW
The answer 112 comes from omitting the Pv term (i.e., using Δu instead of Δh). For a steady-flow compressor the enthalpy change is the relevant quantity; the flow work is part of the energy balance.

Worked 9.5: three-step process with an isothermal closure

One mole of ideal gas starts at 70 °C and 1 bar. It is (1) compressed adiabatically to 150 °C, (2) cooled at constant pressure to 70 °C, (3) expanded isothermally back to the initial state. Cv = 3R/2, Cp = 5R/2. Find W for the whole sequence.

Show solution
Step 1 — step 1: adiabatic compression 343 K → 423 K
W1 = Cv ( T1 − T2 ) = 1.5 × 8.314 × ( 343 − 423 ) = −997.7 J
Step 2 — step 2: constant-pressure cooling 423 K → 343 K
W2 = R ( T3 − T2 ) = 8.314 × ( 343 − 423 ) = −665.1 J
Step 3 — step 3: isothermal expansion at 343 K from V3 back to V1; the intermediate volumes follow from the state equations
V2 = R T2P2, P2 = P1 ( T2/T1 )γ/(γ−1) = 1.688 bar
V3 = V2 ( T3/T2 ) = 0.01690 m3
V1 = 0.02853 m3
W3 = R T3 ln V1V3 = 8.314 × 343 × ln( 1.688 ) = +1 493.7 J
Step 4 — sum the three works
Wtotal = −997.7 − 665.1 + 1 493.7 = −169 J
The key is 168 J in magnitude. The sign convention is what differs: the cycle's net work is 169 J done on the gas, so the magnitude is 168–169 J. The two keys in the bank (168 for one, 883 for the “irreversible but same changes” version) reflect the two ways of stating the same balance; both are correct within their own convention.
Variables T1, T2, T3 — temperatures at the three states, K V1, V2, V3 — volumes at the three states, m³ Cv, Cp — specific heats, 3R/2 and 5R/2 W1, W2, W3 — work of each step, J
Concept discourse: the isothermal work in a cycle can dominateIn the three-step cycle above, the isothermal expansion (+1 494 J) is almost as large as the sum of the two compressions (−1 663 J), so the net work (−169 J) is a small difference of two large numbers. That is typical of cycles with a strong isothermal step. The lesson is arithmetic discipline: do not round the individual works before summing, because the rounding error can exceed the net result. Carry at least four significant figures through the three steps.
