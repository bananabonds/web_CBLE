1. Combination and Permutation

Two questions decide every counting problem: does order matter? and is repetition allowed? The answers fix which formula applies.

Order matters?Repetition?Count
YesNonPr = n! / (n − r)!
YesYesnr
NoNonCr = n! / [ r! (n − r)! ]
NoYesn+r−1Cr

The fundamental counting principle underlies all four: if a task can be done in m ways and a second in n ways, the two together can be done in m × n ways. Multiplying the choices at each stage gives the answer without any formula, and it is the check that catches a wrong formula.

Two special cases are worth naming. A circular permutation of n distinct objects has (n − 1)! arrangements, because rotations of the circle are not distinguished. And when a word contains repeated letters, the number of distinct arrangements is n! divided by the factorial of each repeat count:

arrangements = n!n1! n2! …

The complement is often faster than the direct count. “At least one” is 1 minus “none”; “at least two” is the sum of the exact cases, or 1 minus the cases with zero and one.

Concept discourse: the commonest error is the wrong formula, not the arithmeticNearly every wrong answer in a counting problem comes from applying a permutation when a combination applies, or the reverse. The test is a single question: if you swap two of the chosen items, do you get a different outcome? If yes, order matters and the answer is a permutation. If no, it is a combination. For a committee, swapping members gives the same committee — combination. For a queue, swapping positions gives a different queue — permutation. For a set of digits forming a number, swapping digits gives a different number — permutation. Answer that question before touching a formula.

2. Polynomials

A polynomial in x is a sum of powers with constant coefficients. Three tools carry almost every question in the chapter.

The quadratic and its discriminant

ax² + bx + c = 0, x = −b ± √( b² − 4ac )2a

The quantity under the radical is the discriminant, and its sign alone tells the nature of the roots:

Discriminant D = b² − 4acNature of roots
D > 0two distinct real roots
D = 0one repeated real root (equal roots)
D < 0two complex conjugate roots, no real root

Before computing the discriminant, put the equation in standard form ax² + bx + c = 0. Every sign error in this chapter starts with a term left on the wrong side.

Vieta's formulas

For ax² + bx + c = 0 with roots r and s:

r + s = −ba, rs = ca

For the cubic x³ + px² + qx + r = 0 with roots r, s, t:

r + s + t = −p, rs + rt + st = q, rst = −r

Vieta converts a symmetric expression in the roots into the coefficients, without ever solving for the roots. The most useful example is the sum of reciprocals:

1r + 1s = r + srs

Forming a new equation from old roots

If the roots of a new equation are a function of the old roots — reciprocals, negatives, squares — the new equation is found by the corresponding substitution. The three that recur:

reciprocal roots: replace x by 1/x
negated roots: replace x by −x
scaled roots: replace x by x/k

A subtlety worth knowing: replacing x by 1/x gives a rational expression, which must be multiplied through by x² to restore a polynomial. That multiplication is where sign errors appear.

Functions

An equation represents y as a function of x only if each x gives at most one y. The graphical test is the vertical line test: a curve is a function if no vertical line meets it twice. An equation containing y² is an immediate suspect, because y² = (something) has two branches, y = ±√(something).

Adjacent skills: exponents and logarithms

Equations with unknown exponents are solved by writing every base as a power of one common base, then equating exponents:

am = an ⇒ m = n

And the logarithm of the reciprocal base is the reciprocal of the logarithm:

loga b = 1logb a
Worked 2.1: the discriminant and the nature of the roots

What is the discriminant of 4x² = 8x − 5?

Show solution
Step 1 — put the equation in standard form by moving every term to one side
4x² − 8x + 5 = 0
Step 2 — read off the coefficients: a = 4, b = −8, c = 5
a = 4, b = −8, c = 5
Step 3 — apply the discriminant formula
D = b² − 4ac = ( −8 )² − 4( 4 )( 5 )
Step 4 — evaluate
D = 64 − 80 = −16
The answer 8 comes from forgetting the 4ac term and using b² − 4 = 60… or, more commonly, from a sign slip that turns −4ac into +4ac. The negative discriminant means the two roots are complex conjugates — the parabola never crosses the x-axis.

Worked 2.2: the equation whose roots are reciprocals

Find the equation whose roots are the reciprocals of the roots of 2x² − 3x − 5 = 0.

Show solution
Step 1 — substitute x = 1/y into the original equation
2( 1y )² − 3( 1y ) − 5 = 0
Step 2 — write it out
2y² − 3y − 5 = 0
Step 3 — multiply through by y² to restore a polynomial
2 − 3y − 5y² = 0
Step 4 — rearrange into standard order with a positive leading coefficient
5y² + 3y − 2 = 0
Step 5 — rename the variable back to x
5x² + 3x − 2 = 0
The answer 2x² + 3x − 5 = 0 keeps the original leading coefficient and only flips the middle sign — it is the equation with negated middle coefficient, not reciprocal roots. The correct transformation reverses the coefficients: 2, −3, −5 becomes 5, 3, −2 (the old coefficients in reverse order, with an overall sign flip). That reversal is the fast check.
Variables x — variable of the original equation y — variable of the new equation 2, −3, −5 — coefficients of the original quadratic

Worked 2.7: a system of exponential equations

Solve for x: 27x = 9y and 81y · 3−x = 243.

Show solution
Step 1 — write every base as a power of 3
27 = 3³, 9 = 3², 81 = 3⁴, 243 = 3⁵
Step 2 — rewrite the first equation

Step 3 — rewrite the second equation

Worked 2.8: which equation does not define y as a function of x

Which of the following does not represent y as a function of x? (A) x³ + y² = 1 (B) y = x² (C) y = √x (D) all of the above.

Show solution
Step 1 — apply the vertical-line test: a relation is a function if every vertical line meets the graph at most once
at most one y for each x
Step 2 — solve (A) for y
y² = 1 − x³ ⇒ y = ± √( 1 − x³ )
Step 3 — two branches for the same x: not a function
(A) fails the test
Step 4 — check the others
(B) y = x² gives one y per x ✓
(C) y = √x gives one y per x (the principal root) ✓
Answer: (A)
Any equation in which y appears only to an even power is an immediate suspect, because an even power has two branches. The answer “all of the above” is the trap: (B) and (C) are perfectly good functions.
Variables x — independent variable y — dependent variable
Worked 2.9: an exponent equation with a unit base

Find all real x such that [ (1/5)(x² − 10x + 26) ]x² − 6x + 5 = 1.

Show solution
Step 1 — a positive base raised to a power equals 1 in exactly two ways: the exponent is zero (and the base is not zero), or the base itself is 1
case 1: exponent = 0; case 2: base = 1
Step 2 — case 1: solve the exponent equation
x² − 6x + 5 = 0 ⇒ ( x − 1 )( x − 5 ) = 0
x = 1, 5
Step 3 — case 2: solve the base equation

Step 4 — check that the base is never zero or negative at any of these values: x² − 10x + 26 = (x − 5)² + 1 ≥ 1, so the base is at least 1/5 > 0 everywhere

Concept discourse: radicals with fractional exponentsThe radical-and-exponent item in this set is worth separating out because the recorded expression does not simplify to the keyed answer under the natural reading. Writing ∛(ab) as (ab)1/3 and √(ab) as (ab)1/2, the expression √( ab / ∛(ab) ) evaluates to (ab)1/2 − 1/3 = (ab)1/6, the sixth root — not ∛(ab). To reach ∛(ab) the grouping would have to differ, for example √(ab) divided by the sixth root of ab, or a denominator written as (ab)1/6. The rule itself is unambiguous and worth memorising: a radical over a radical subtracts exponents, a radical times a radical adds them, and a power of a power multiplies them. Apply the rule to the expression as printed and compare with the options — if none matches, the stem's grouping has been lost in transcription and the printed key is not a safe target. 3. Series

Two sequences cover the whole chapter: the arithmetic progression, where consecutive terms differ by a constant, and the geometric progression, where consecutive terms differ by a constant ratio.

Arithmetic progression

an = a1 + ( n − 1 ) d
Sn = n2 [ 2a1 + ( n − 1 ) d ] = n2 ( a1 + an )

The second form of the sum is the one to use when the first and last terms are known; the first is used when d is known. When the number of terms is odd, the sum is simply the number of terms times the middle term — a shortcut that saves a full calculation.

Geometric progression

an = a1 rn−1
Sn = a1 rn − 1r − 1 ( r ≠ 1 )
S∞ = a11 − r ( | r | < 1 )

The common ratio is found from the ratio of two known terms, and the exponent difference tells you how many multiplications of r are involved:

aman = rm−n

A geometric mean inserted between two numbers preserves the common ratio. For k geometric means between a and b, the total number of terms is k + 2, and the ratio is the (k+1)-th root of b/a.

Arithmetic sequences hiding in word problems

A list of numbers that are “divisible by d between L and U” is an arithmetic progression. The first term is the smallest multiple of d at or above L, the last is the largest multiple at or below U, and the count follows from the difference of their multipliers:

count = last multipled − first multipled + 1

Recognising that structure turns a word problem into two lines of arithmetic.

Find the 30th term of the arithmetic progression 4, 7, 10, …

Show solution
Step 1 — identify the first term and the common difference

Worked 3.6: a sum disguised as a divisibility problem

Find the sum of all integers between 300 and 800 that are exactly divisible by 14.

Show solution

Concept discourse: counting terms is where AP sums failThe sum formula is easy; the count is where marks are lost. Two rules cover every case. First, the first term is the smallest value that satisfies the condition and lies inside the range — a multiple just below the lower bound is not a term. Second, the number of terms is (last − first)/d + 1, and that final +1 is not optional: it counts both endpoints. Converting to multipliers — “14 × 22 through 14 × 57” — makes the +1 arithmetic on small integers and removes the risk of a slip in the thousands.
