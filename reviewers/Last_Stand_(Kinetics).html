1. Reaction Order

The order of a reaction is the exponent of each concentration in the rate law. For a reaction with a single reactant A the empirical rate law is

−rA = k CAn

and the order n decides the shape of the concentration–time curve. The standard diagnostic is the ratio method: take two experiments in which only CA0 changes and compare the initial rates.

n = ln( r2 / r1 )ln( C2 / C1 )

When the data are a table of concentration against time, the diagnostic is to test each integrated form. The three that matter:

OrderIntegrated formLinear plot
0CA = CA0 − k tCA against t
1ln CA = ln CA0 − k tln CA against t
21/CA = 1/CA0 + k t1/CA against t

A constant decrement in concentration per equal time interval is zero order; a constant ratio is first order; a constant increment of 1/C is second order. Testing the increment is faster than plotting, and it catches the answer on the first pass.

Two special cases are examinable. Pseudo-first-order reactions occur when one reactant is in large excess, so its concentration is effectively constant and the observed order drops. Cane-sugar inversion (sucrose → glucose + fructose) is bimolecular in principle but first order in practice, because water is the solvent and its concentration does not change. And the zero-order constant-volume bomb has a pressure relation

π = pp0 = 1 + ( r − 1 ) k tCA0

which is linear in t and therefore first-order-looking in π but zero-order in concentration.

Show solution

Concept discourse: pseudo-first-order reactionsCane-sugar inversion is the classic pseudo-first-order reaction. The stoichiometry is bimolecular (sucrose + water → glucose + fructose), but water is the solvent and its concentration is essentially constant over the reaction. The observed rate is first order in sucrose alone. Questions that ask “what is the order” and offer “bimolecular reaction with first order” are testing whether you distinguish the molecularity (bimolecular) from the order (first). The order is what the rate law shows; the molecularity is what the mechanism shows. They need not agree.

2. Rate Law

A rate law is the empirical equation that relates the reaction rate to the concentrations. The task is to find the constant k and the exponents from data, then use the resulting equation to predict concentrations.

For a first-order reaction with variable total moles, the constant-volume bomb relation is

CA = CA0 e−k t, π = 1 + ( Δn1 ) X

where Δn is the increase in moles per mole of A reacted. For A → 2.4R, Δn = 1.4, and the conversion follows directly from the pressure rise:

X = π − 1Δn

For a second-order reaction the integrated form is used to compute concentrations at any time:

1CA = 1CA0 + k t

The serial reaction A → B → C has its own rate law for the intermediate B:

CB = CA0 k1k2 − k1 ( e−k1t − e−k2t )

The maximum concentration of B is reached at the optimum time

tmax = ln( k2 / k1 )k2 − k1

and the maximum value is

CB,max = CA0 ( k1k2 )k2/(k2−k1)
Worked 2.1: conversion in a constant-volume bomb

A slow gas-phase reaction A → 2.4R with first-order kinetics runs isothermally at 25 °C in a constant-volume bomb. Starting with pure A at 1 atm, the pressure rises to 1.8 atm in 75 min. Find the actual conversion of A.

Show solution
Step 1 — the moles increase by 1.4 per mole of A reacted
Δn = 2.4 − 1 = 1.4
Step 2 — relate pressure rise to conversion
π = 1 + Δn X
1.8 = 1 + 1.4 X
Step 3 — solve for X
X = 0.81.4 = 0.571 ≈ 0.57
The answer 0.75 comes from using Δn = 1 (forgetting the 1.4 factor). The pressure rise is proportional to the change in moles, not to the total product moles.
Variables π — reduced pressure, 1.8 Δn — increase in moles per mole of A reacted, 1.4 X — conversion of A

3. Half-Life

The half-life t1/2 is the time for the reactant concentration to fall to half its value. For an n-th order reaction with n ≠ 1 it varies with initial concentration:

t1/2 = 2n−1 − 1( n − 1 ) k CA0n−1

For first order the half-life is independent of CA0:

t1/2 = ln 2k

The concentration dependence is the diagnostic. Taking logarithms of the general form gives

ln t1/2 = constant − ( n − 1 ) ln CA0

so a plot of ln t1/2 against ln CA0 has slope (1 − n). Two data points give

n = 1 + ln( t1/2,1 / t1/2,2 )ln( C2 / C1 )

For zero order the half-life is proportional to CA0; for first order it is constant; for second order it is inversely proportional to CA0. A half-life that decreases as CA0 increases means n > 1; a half-life that increases means n < 1.

Worked 3.1: order from two half-lives

Half-life data for the irreversible liquid-phase reaction A → P: CA0 = 2 kmol/m³ gives t1/2 = 2 min; CA0 = 8 kmol/m³ gives t1/2 = 1 min. Find the overall order.

Show solution
Step 1 — write the half-life relation for order n
t1/2 ∝ CA01−n
Step 2 — take the ratio of the two data points
t1/2,1t1/2,2 = ( C1C2 )1−n
21 = ( 28 )1−n = ( 14 )1−n = 4n−1
Step 3 — solve the exponent
n − 1 = ln 2ln 4 = 0.69311.3863 = 0.5
n = 1.5
The answer 1 would require a constant half-life (first order), which the data contradict. The answer 2 would require t1/2 to fall by a factor of 4 when CA0 rises by a factor of 4; the observed fall is by a factor of 2, which is the 1.5-order signature.
Variables C1, C2 — initial concentrations, kmol/m³ t1/2,1, t1/2,2 — half-lives, min n — reaction order

4. Reaction Constant

The reaction rate constant k is found by fitting the appropriate integrated form to the concentration–time data, after the order has been established. For the first-order and second-order forms the data are plotted or differenced directly; for the serial reaction the two rate constants follow from the time to maximum intermediate.

Two standard forms dominate the exam:

first order: k = 1t ln CA0CA
second order: k = 1t ( 1CA − 1CA0 )

For the serial reaction A → B → C, the time to maximum intermediate is

tmax = ln( k2 / k1 )k2 − k1

Given k1 and tmax, the equation must be solved for k2 by trial. The approximate reading is that k2 is much larger or much smaller than k1 depending on whether tmax is small or large relative to 1/k1.

For a reaction whose order is not known, the first step is to identify the order by the diagnostic in Chapter 1, then apply the corresponding integrated form. Using the wrong integrated form is the standard error — it produces a k that varies with time, which is the signal that the order was guessed wrong.

5. Activation Energy

The temperature dependence of the rate constant is described by the Arrhenius equation:

k = k0 e−Ea/RT

Taking logarithms between two temperatures gives the working form:

ln k2k1 = EaR ( 1T1 − 1T2 )

Three qualitative results follow and each is examinable.

  • k increases with temperature. The rate constant never decreases with rising T.
  • The frequency factor k0 does not affect the temperature sensitivity. The sensitivity is governed entirely by Ea, because ln(k2/k1) is independent of k0: the k0 cancels when the ratio is formed.
  • Reactions are more temperature-sensitive at low temperature than at high temperature, because the same Ea corresponds to a larger fractional change in k when T is small.

For transition-state theory the pre-exponential factor carries an extra T, so

k ∝ T e−E0/RT

and the ratio between two temperatures is the Arrhenius ratio multiplied by the ratio of temperatures:

k2k1TST = T2T1 k2k1Arr
Worked 5.1: activation energy from two conversions

A second-order reaction with reactants initially at 0.10 mol/L is 20 % complete in 40 min at 25 °C, and 40 % complete in 35 min at 50 °C. Find the activation energy in cal/mol.

Show solution
Step 1 — rate constant at 25 °C from the second-order form
k1 = 1t ( 1C − 1C0 ) = 140 ( 10.08 − 10.10 )
k1 = 2.540 = 0.0625 L/mol·min
Step 2 — rate constant at 50 °C
k2 = 135 ( 10.06 − 10.10 ) = 6.66735 = 0.1905 L/mol·min
Step 3 — apply the Arrhenius two-temperature form
ln 0.19050.0625 = Ea1.987 ( 1298 − 1323 )
Step 4 — evaluate the two sides
ln( 3.048 ) = 1.115
1298 − 1323 = 3.356×10−3 − 3.096×10−3 = 2.597×10−4
Step 5 — solve for Ea
Ea = 1.115 × 1.9872.597×10−4 = 8 531 cal/mol ≈ 8 500 cal/mol
The answer 2 700 cal/mol is what comes from using the first-order form for k, which changes both k values and therefore the ratio. The order diagnostic must come first; only then can the activation energy be computed.
Variables k1, k2 — second-order rate constants at the two temperatures T1, T2 — absolute temperatures, 298 and 323 K Ea — activation energy, cal/mol R — gas constant, 1.987 cal/mol·K
Worked 5.2: transition-state versus Arrhenius ratio

At a given value of E/R, the ratio of the rate constants at 500 K and 400 K is 2 if the Arrhenius law is used. What is the ratio if transition-state theory is used with the same value of E/R?

Show solution

2 = exp[ ER ( 1400 − 1500 ) ] = exp[ ER × 5×10−4 ]

Step 2 — transition-state theory adds a factor of T2/T1

The answer 2 is the Arrhenius ratio, not the TST ratio. The TST ratio carries the extra T2/T1 factor, which is 1.25 here.
Variables E/R — activation energy divided by the gas constant, K T1, T2 — the two absolute temperatures, K
Concept discourse: what the frequency factor does and does not controlThe Arrhenius equation has two parameters, k0 and Ea. The ratio k2/k1 at two temperatures is exp[Ea/R(1/T1 − 1/T2)], which does not contain k0 at all. The frequency factor sets the magnitude of k at any given temperature, but it does not set how k changes with T. That is the point of the statement “the value of the frequency factor k0 does not affect the temperature sensitivity”, and it is the statement the exam marks as true. The wrong options in this question type claim the opposite, or claim that a reaction is more T-sensitive at high T (it is less sensitive, because at high T the exponential is already near 1).

6. Reactor Types

Three ideal reactor models cover the exam. The differences are in how the concentration varies inside the reactor.

ReactorConcentration historyDesign equation
BatchAll properties vary with time; no flow in or outt = NA0 ∫ dX/(−rA V)
Mixed flow (CSTR)Uniform at the exit condition throughoutV = FA0 X/(−rA)
Plug flow (PFR)Varies along the length; no radial gradientV = FA0 ∫ dX/(−rA)

The batch reactor is characterized by the variation of extent of reaction and properties with time. It has no residence time in the continuous sense; it has a reaction time. The single most examinable statement is that the batch reactor's composition varies with time, not with position and not with a constant residence time.

For a reaction of order n > 0 the PFR always gives higher conversion than a CSTR of the same volume, because the PFR exploits the higher concentration near the entrance. For zero order the two are identical, because the rate does not depend on concentration.

The space time τ = V/v0 is the time to process one reactor volume of feed at the inlet conditions. The space velocity is its reciprocal, v0/V. Some textbooks use “space velocity” to mean V/v0; the units (min⁻¹ vs min) tell you which convention is in play, and reading the units before substituting is essential.

Worked 6.1: batch versus CSTR versus PFR for zero order

A liquid-phase zero-order irreversible reaction A → B gives 0.3 conversion in a CSTR at a space velocity of 0.1 min⁻¹. Find the conversion in a PFR at a space velocity of 0.2 min⁻¹.

Show solution
Step 1 — interpret the space velocity as V/v0, so that the space time is numerically the given value
τCSTR = 0.1 min, τPFR = 0.2 min
Step 2 — zero-order CSTR: X = k τ / CA0
0.3 = k × 0.1CA0 ⇒ kCA0 = 3 min−1
Step 3 — zero-order PFR has the same linear form: X = k τ / CA0
XPFR = 3 × 0.2 = 0.6
The answer 0.30 would be right only if the space velocity were doubled but the conversion were first-order and small; for a zero-order reaction the conversion is linear in the space time, so doubling τ doubles X. The units of “space velocity” decide the arithmetic: if the convention is v0/V (reciprocal of space time), then τCSTR = 10 min and τPFR = 5 min, and the same calculation gives XPFR = 0.15 instead. Check the unit before substituting. See the flags table.
Variables X — conversion of A k — zero-order rate constant, mol/L·min CA0 — inlet concentration, mol/L τ — space time, min
Concept discourse: what makes a batch reactor a batch reactorThe distinguishing feature is that nothing flows in or out, so the composition changes with time as the reaction proceeds. A question that offers “constant residence time” is describing a CSTR or a PFR, not a batch. A question that offers “the variation in extent of reaction and properties of the reaction mixture with time” is describing exactly what makes a batch reactor what it is. The distinction is conceptual, not algebraic.

7. Reactor Volume

The design equations convert a required conversion into a reactor volume. For the three ideal reactors:

CSTR: V = FA0 X−rA
PFR: V = FA0 ∫0X dX−rA

For constant-density systems (liquids) the rate is written in terms of conversion with CA = CA0(1 − X). For gas-phase systems the density changes with conversion, and the expansion factor appears:

CA = CA0 1 − X1 + εX, ε = yA0 δ

with δ the change in moles per mole of A reacted. The first-order variable-density PFR integral is

V = v0k [ ( 1 + ε ) ln 11 − X − ε X ]

The scaling question — “by what factor must V change when the inlet conditions change?” — is best answered in ratio form, so that k cancels. For a first-order CSTR:

V ∝ v0 CA0 − CACA

which shows that V scales linearly with the flow and with the amount removed, inversely with the outlet concentration.

Worked 7.1: scaling a CSTR when the feed and flow change

A pollutant degrades by first-order kinetics. A stream with 2 kmol/m³ of pollutant at 1 m³/h needs a CSTR of volume V to reach 0.5 kmol/m³. The inlet concentration is then doubled and the flow is tripled, while the outlet must still be 0.5 kmol/m³. By what factor must the reactor volume increase?

Show solution
Step 1 — write the first-order CSTR design equation in ratio form
V = v0 ( CA0 − CA )k CA
Step 2 — evaluate the original volume symbolically
V1 = (1)(2 − 0.5)k (0.5) = 3k
Step 3 — evaluate the new volume
V2 = (3)(4 − 0.5)k (0.5) = 21k
Step 4 — take the ratio
V2V1 = 21/k3/k = 7
The factor 7 is the product of the flow factor 3 and the removal factor (3.5/1.5 = 7/3). The wrong 7/2 uses only one of the two changes.
Variables V1, V2 — original and new volumes v0 — volumetric flow, m³/h CA0 — inlet concentration, kmol/m³ CA — outlet concentration, kmol/m³ k — first-order rate constant, h⁻¹
Worked 7.2: gas-phase PFR with expansion

A homogeneous gas-phase reaction A → 3R has −rA = 10⁻² CA0.5 mol/L·s at 215 °C. Find the space time for 80 % conversion of a 50 % A / 50 % inert feed in a PFR at 215 °C and 5 atm.

Show solution
Step 1 — expansion factor
δ = 3 − 1 = 2, ε = yA0 δ = 0.5 × 2 = 1.0
Step 2 — inlet concentration from the ideal gas law, with P in atm and R in L·atm/mol·K
CA0 = yA0 PR T = 0.5 × 50.08206 × 488.15 = 0.0624 mol/L
Step 3 — the variable-density PFR space time
τ = CA0k ∫0X dXCA00.5 [ (1 − X)/(1 + εX) ]0.5
Step 4 — simplify the constants

Worked 7.4: zero-order constant-pressure volume

For a zero-order reaction A → rR in a constant-volume bomb, π = 1 at t = 0 and π = 1.5 at t = 1. In a constant-pressure setup, find V at t = 1 if V = 1 at t = 0.

Show solution
Step 1 — from the bomb data, find the group (r − 1)k/CA0
π = 1 + ( r − 1 ) k tCA0
1.5 = 1 + ( r − 1 ) k (1)CA0 ⇒ ( r − 1 ) kCA0 = 0.5
Step 2 — constant pressure: the volume follows the total moles, which grow exponentially in the zero-order case
VV0 = exp[ ( r − 1 ) k tCA0 ]
Step 3 — substitute the group at t = 1
VV0 = exp( 0.5 × 1 ) = e0.5 = 1.6487
V ≈ 1.65 V0
The answer does not depend on r, which is why r is not given. The linear extrapolation to 1.5 V0 is the natural wrong answer; the constant-pressure zero-order case grows exponentially because the volume increases as the reaction proceeds, which accelerates the consumption.
Variables π — reduced pressure, p/p0 r — moles of product per mole of A reacted k — zero-order rate constant, mol/L·s t — time, s V, V0 — current and initial volumes
Concept discourse: why the PFR and CSTR coincide for zero orderThe PFR integral ∫ dX/(−rA) is the area under the curve of 1/(−rA) against X. The CSTR formula is the rectangle of height 1/(−rA)exit and width X. For a zero-order rate the curve is horizontal, so the area and the rectangle are identical. For any positive-order rate the curve rises as X increases, so the area is smaller than the rectangle, and the PFR requires less volume. That is the geometric explanation of a result that is otherwise just algebra.
