1. Simple Interest

Simple interest accrues only on the original principal, never on the interest already accumulated. The three quantities are related by

I = P r t
F = P ( 1 + r t )

where t is in years. When t is given as a date range, the number of days must be counted, and the exact method uses 365 days in the denominator while the ordinary (banker's) method uses 360. The difference is about 1.4 % and matters in date-range problems.

Two day-counting conventions appear in the exam:

ConventionDenominatorUse
Exact simple interest365Most textbook problems
Ordinary simple interest360Banking practice

Counting the days is mechanical but easy to get wrong. List each month's contribution, subtract the starting day from the ending day within the same month, and check the total against the calendar. The month contributions for a date range that begins on the 21st of one month and ends on the 25th of a later month are: (days remaining in the first month) + (full days in each intervening month) + (days used in the last month).

2. Compound and Continuous Interest

Compound interest accrues on the principal plus the accumulated interest. For a nominal annual rate r compounded m times per year over n years:

F = P ( 1 + rm )m n

For continuous compounding (m → ∞):

F = P er t

The two are linked by the limit (1 + r/m)m → er. Continuous compounding gives the largest possible future value for a given nominal rate, because it compounds at every instant.

The compounding count is the trap in this chapter. "Bimonthly" in engineering-economics practice means six times per year (every two months), not twelve times per year (twice a month). Read the frequency, decide m before touching the formula, and write m on its own line.

3. Effective vs Nominal Rate

The nominal rate is the quoted annual rate before compounding. The effective annual rate is the actual percentage growth over one year, including all compoundings within it:

reff = ( 1 + rm )m − 1

For continuous compounding:

reff = er − 1

The effective rate is always greater than or equal to the nominal rate, with equality only at m = 1 (annual compounding). Two loans quoted at the same nominal rate can have different effective rates if their compounding frequencies differ.

Finding the effective rate from a growth over a period

If a principal P grows to F over t years, the effective annual rate satisfies

F = P ( 1 + reff )t ⇒ reff = ( FP )1/t − 1

This is the single-period form of the compound-interest equation, and it is what a "what is the effective rate" question wants whenever the growth is given directly.

4. Discount Rate and Banker's Discount

A discounted loan is one in which the interest is deducted in advance: the borrower receives less than the face value and repays the face. Two rates describe such a loan.

The discount rate (also called the banker's discount rate) is the discount as a fraction of the face value:

d = F − PF × 1t

The interest rate is the discount as a fraction of the proceeds P:

r = F − PP × 1t

Because P < F, the interest rate is always larger than the discount rate:

r = d1 − d t

The discount rate is the customary way to quote short-term commercial paper, and the interest rate is the way to compare the loan against other investments.

Worked 4.1: banker's discount on a 9-month loan

Ms. Evilla borrows from a bank. She receives P1 340 and promises to pay P1 500 at the end of 9 months. Determine the corresponding discount rate.

Step 2 — convert 9 months to years

Both conventions are in circulation: one annualizes (14.22 %), one reports the period rate (10.67 %). The two rates describe the same loan; the question must be read to see which is being asked. The interest rate on the proceeds would be 160/1340 = 11.94 % for the period, or 15.92 % annualized. See the flags table.

5. Annuities

An annuity is a series of equal payments at equal intervals. The two forms are:

  • Ordinary annuity — payments at the end of each period. This is the default form of the standard annuity factors.
  • Annuity due — payments at the beginning of each period. Every payment is one period earlier than in the ordinary case, so the present value is multiplied by (1 + i) and the future value likewise:
PVdue = PVordinary × ( 1 + i )
FVdue = FVordinary × ( 1 + i )

The standard factors:

P/A: P = A ( 1 + i )n − 1i ( 1 + i )n
A/P: A = P i ( 1 + i )n( 1 + i )n − 1
F/A: F = A ( 1 + i )n − 1i
A/F: A = F i( 1 + i )n − 1

The four factors are reciprocals in pairs: P/A and A/P, F/A and A/F. A table that gives one of each pair is enough.

A deferred annuity is an ordinary annuity whose first payment is delayed by k periods. Its present value is the ordinary present value at the start of the annuity, discounted back k more periods:

PVdeferred = A ( P/A, i, n ) ( P/F, i, k )

where n is the number of payments and k is the number of periods of deferral.

6. Gradients

A gradient is an annuity whose payments change by a fixed amount (arithmetic) or a fixed percentage (geometric) each period.

Arithmetic gradient

For a series that starts at A in period 1 and increases by G each period through period n:

P = A ( P/A, i, n ) + G ( P/G, i, n )
( P/G, i, n ) = ( 1 + i )n − i n − 1i² ( 1 + i )n

The gradient factor converts a series of increments 0, G, 2G, …, (n−1)G into a single present worth. Because the first increment is zero, the factor is applied to the (n−1)th period's rise, not the nth.

Geometric gradient

For a series that starts at A and grows by a fixed percentage g each period:

P = A 1 − ( 1 + g )n ( 1 + i )−ni − g ( g ≠ i )

When g = i the expression reduces to P = nA/(1 + i). The geometric gradient is the arithmetic gradient's percentage cousin: an arithmetic gradient adds a constant amount, a geometric gradient multiplies by a constant ratio.

7. Amortization

An amortized loan is repaid by a series of equal payments, each of which covers the interest due on the outstanding balance and reduces the principal by the remainder. The constant payment is the (A/P) factor applied to the original principal:

A = P ( A/P, i, n ) = P i ( 1 + i )n( 1 + i )n − 1

The period rate i is the nominal rate divided by the compounding frequency. If the rate is quoted as effective annual, the period rate is (1 + reff)1/m − 1, as in Worked 5.2.

Two kinds of amortization statement are examinable. The interest portion of any payment equals the period rate times the outstanding balance at the start of the period. The principal portion is the payment minus the interest portion. The outstanding balance after any payment equals the present value of the remaining payments.

8. Depreciation: Straight Line

Straight-line depreciation charges the same amount each year:

d = FC − SVn
BVk = FC − k d

The first cost FC includes the purchase price and every cost necessary to put the asset into service (freight, installation, foundations). The salvage value SV is the estimated residual value at the end of the life. Both must be in the same currency and at the same point in time.

The first-cost trap

A problem that says "salvage value is 10 % of first cost" is ambiguous unless first cost is defined. In engineering economics, "first cost" conventionally means the installed cost — the purchase price plus freight, installation and any other costs of putting the asset into service. So for a machine bought for P500 000 with P30 000 of installation, the first cost is P530 000, and 10 % of that is P53 000, not P50 000. Reading "first cost" as "purchase price" is the commonest error in this chapter.

9. Depreciation: Sinking Fund

The sinking-fund method sets aside an equal annual amount that, with compound interest, accumulates to the total depreciation at the end of the life. The annual deposit is

d = ( FC − SV ) i( 1 + i )n − 1 = ( FC − SV )( A/F, i, n )

The book value after k years is the first cost minus the accumulated fund:

BVk = FC − ( FC − SV ) ( 1 + i )k − 1( 1 + i )n − 1

The total depreciation after k years is the accumulated amount of the k deposits:

Dk = d ( F/A, i, k ) = d ( 1 + i )k − 1i

The sinking-fund and straight-line methods both start and end at the same book value, but the sinking-fund book value is higher throughout the life, because the fund grows. That is why the sinking-fund charge is larger than the straight-line charge early on: it must both accumulate and grow.

10. Depreciation: Sum of Years Digits (SOYD)

The sum-of-years-digits method is an accelerated depreciation: larger charges in the early years, smaller in the late years. The annual charge in year k of an n-year life is

dk = ( FC − SV ) n − k + 1S, S = n ( n + 1 )2

The sum-of-years digits S is the sum 1 + 2 + … + n. The numerator n − k + 1 counts the years remaining at the start of year k: year 1 gets n, year 2 gets n − 1, and the last year gets 1.

Total depreciation after k years

The cumulative depreciation after k years is

Dk = ( FC − SV ) k ( 2n − k + 1 )2 S

Equivalently, sum the annual charges for the first k years: for n = 10 and k = 3, the sum of numerators is 10 + 9 + 8 = 27, over S = 55, giving D3 = 27/55 × (FC − SV).

The sign and count check

Every SOYD calculation has two easy checks. First, the sum of all annual fractions must equal 1: n/S + (n−1)/S + … + 1/S = S/S = 1, so the total depreciation over the full life is exactly FC − SV. Second, the annual fraction must decrease each year: d1 > d2 > … > dn. If either check fails, an index is off by one.
