1. Crystallization

A crystallizer produces solid crystals from a solution by cooling, evaporating, or adding a salting-out agent. Three calculations dominate the chapter, and they are always done in the same order.

  • Solubility balance — the solubility curve gives the saturation composition at each temperature. On a dry-solvent basis the numbers can be read directly from the table.
  • Yield balance — the solute in the feed equals the solute left in the mother liquor plus the solute in the crystals. The crystals may be hydrated, so the water of hydration has to be accounted for.
  • Heat balance — the heat to be removed is the sensible cooling of the solution plus the heat of crystallization. The heat-transfer area then follows from Q = U A ΔT.

The single most important convention is the basis. Solubility tables are usually given as kg anhydrous solute per 100 kg water (or per 100 kg solvent). A hydrated crystal contains solute plus water of hydration, so when the crystal is weighed as the hydrate, the water of crystallization comes out of the mother liquor and must be added to the solute-balance equation.

solute in feed = solute in mother liquor + solute in crystals
water in feed = water in mother liquor + water of crystallization

For a hydrate of formula M·nH2O with molecular masses Ma (anhydrous) and Mh (hydrate), the mass fraction of anhydrous solute in the hydrate is

wa = MaMh

and the rest is water of crystallization. For Na2CO3·10H2O, wa = 106/286 = 0.3706; for Na2SO4·10H2O, wa = 142/322 = 0.4410; for CH3COONa·3H2O, wa = 82/136 = 0.603.

Concept discourse: the water of crystallisation is not optionalEvery hydrate problem turns on the fact that the crystal takes its water out of the mother liquor. Forgetting this term makes the mother liquor appear to hold more water than it does, which is why the yield always comes out too low (86 % instead of 91 %) and the crystallisation temperature too low (18 °C instead of 26 °C). The two equations that never fail are written in the same order every time: solute balance first, water balance second, both with the hydrate mass C in them. Solving them together is mechanical.

2. Driers (equipment)

The equipment chapter is mostly a catalogue of dryer types and their fields of application. The examinable content is the match between a dryer and a material — not a formula.

TypeHow it worksBest suited toNot suited to
Tray / tunnelMaterial spread on trays, hot air passed over it; trays carried through a tunnelSmall batches, heat-sensitive material, pastes and slurriesContinuous high throughput
RotarySloped rotating cylinder with internal flights that lift and shower the material through the hot gasGranular, free-flowing solids; large capacitiesSticky or pasty materials (the flights would cake)
SpraySlurry atomised into a hot-gas chamber; droplets dry before hitting the wallSolutions and slurries producing powders (milk, detergents)Materials that cannot be pumped
Fluidised bedHot gas suspends particles; excellent heat and mass transferFree-flowing particles; rapid uniform dryingWide particle-size distribution
Drum / filmThin film spread on a heated rotating drum; dried and scraped offPast slurries and solutions forming a flaky productHeat-sensitive material
FreezeWater sublimes from the frozen material under vacuumVery heat-sensitive products (pharmaceuticals)Cheap bulk drying

The distinction that the exam loves is the rotary dryer's limitations. The flights (internal lifters) lift the material and shower it through the hot gas — that is what makes a rotary dryer efficient for free-flowing granules. But a sticky material will coat the flights and the shell, so the lifters cannot work, and the dryer must be fitted with chains or replaced by a different type. The classic wrong statement on a rotary-dryer question is “rotary dryer is suitable for drying sticky material” — it is not.

The other examinable point is the direction of gas flow. A rotary dryer can be parallel-flow (gas and solids move in the same direction, so the hottest gas meets the wettest solid — good for heat-sensitive material) or counter-current (gas and solids move opposite, giving a higher exit-solid temperature and better thermal efficiency — good for non-heat-sensitive material).

Concept discourse: read the question as “which statement is false”These equipment questions always ask for the wrong statement, not the right one. Read the stem twice before scanning the options, and use the catalogue above as a checklist. The true statements about a rotary dryer — flights lift the material, parallel or counter-current gas flow, large capacity, simple construction — are all in the first two columns. The false one is always the claim of suitability for a material it cannot handle: sticky, pasty, or caking.

3. Drying (process)

Drying is the removal of moisture from a solid by evaporation into a gas stream. The calculations run in a fixed sequence, and every question in the chapter is a variation on it.

Moisture content

Moisture is reported on either a wet basis (mass water / mass wet solid) or a dry basis (mass water / mass dry solid). The two differ, and the dry basis is the one that makes the mass balance linear:

Xdry = w1 − w, wwet = X1 + X

where w is the wet-basis mass fraction and X is the dry-basis moisture content.

Water removed and product mass

Take a basis of 1 kg of dry solid. The dry-solid mass is unchanged through the dryer, so the mass balance is simply

water removed = ms ( X1 − X2 )
product = ms ( 1 + X2 )

The answer to “how much water is removed per tonne of product” is the first equation divided by the product mass per tonne.

Air requirement from the humidity balance

The air carries the water away. On a dry-air basis the water picked up per kilogram of dry air is the difference of the humidities:

ma ( Yout − Yin ) = water removed

For an adiabatic dryer the air follows a constant wet-bulb line, so the exit humidity is the saturation humidity at the wet-bulb temperature of the entering air, and the exit dry-bulb equals that wet-bulb if the air leaves saturated.

Heat requirement

The heat to be supplied is the heat needed to raise the air from its entering condition to the dryer-inlet temperature, plus any heat of vaporisation not supplied by the air's own sensible cooling. In a well-insulated adiabatic dryer the air cools as it humidifies and the only external heat is the preheater before the dryer:

Qpreheat = ma cs ( Tin − Tambient )

Constant-rate and falling-rate periods

A drying curve has two regimes. During the constant-rate period the surface is fully wet and the rate is set by the external heat and mass transfer. During the falling-rate period the surface begins to dry and the rate is controlled by internal diffusion. The critical moisture content marks the transition, and the equilibrium moisture is the value below which no further drying occurs at the given air conditions.

The time to dry from X1 to X2 in the constant-rate period is proportional to the moisture removed:

t = ms ( X1 − X2 )A Rc

In the falling-rate period the rate falls linearly with moisture content, and the time is a logarithm:

t = ms ( Xc − Xe )A Rc ln X1 − XeX2 − Xe

where Xc is the critical moisture and Xe the equilibrium moisture.

4. Evaporation

An evaporator concentrates a solution by boiling off solvent. Three quantities define the duty:

  • Vapour rate V from the material balance, which depends only on the feed rate and the concentrations.
  • Heat duty Q from the energy balance, which includes heating the feed to the boiling point plus the latent heat of vaporisation.
  • Steam economy E = V / ms, the kilograms of water evaporated per kilogram of steam consumed.

Material balance

F = V + L, F xF = L xL
VF = 1 − xFxL

Energy balance

Q = F cp ( Tb − TF ) + V λ
Q = ms λs

The latent heat λ is evaluated at the boiling temperature of the solution; λs at the steam temperature. The two are different and must be read separately.

Heating surface

Q = U A ΔT, ΔT = Ts − Tb

Multiple effects

In an N-effect train one kilogram of fresh steam evaporates roughly N kilograms of water, because the vapour from effect 1 heats effect 2, and so on. The temperature budget is divided among the effects in inverse proportion to their overall coefficients:

ΔTi = ΔTtot 1/UiΣj 1/Uj, ΔTtot = Ts − Tlast − Σ ΔTbpe

The boiling-point elevation (BPE) is subtracted first, at the cold end; it is the amount by which the solution boils above pure water at the same pressure.

Worked 4.5: temperature split in a four-effect evaporator

Show solution
Step 1 — total available temperature difference after subtracting the BPE

Step 2 — 1/U for each effect
3.390×10−4, 3.745×10−4, 4.831×10−4, 7.353×10−4

Variables ΔTtot — total temperature budget after BPE, K Ui — overall coefficient of effect i, W/m²·K ΔTi — temperature difference across effect i, K T1 — boiling point in effect 1, °C
Concept discourse: the BPE and the temperature budgetThe boiling-point elevation is not a correction to be applied casually at the end of a calculation; it is subtracted from the temperature budget before the split. A 10 K BPE on a 70 K total budget is a 14 % loss of driving force, and distributing the remaining 60 K among four effects correctly depends on that subtraction being done first. In a single-effect calculation the BPE enters twice — once as the difference between the boiling temperature and the vapour-space temperature (which raises the latent heat of the vapour) and once as a reduction of the available ΔT for heat transfer. Missing either use changes the answer by several per cent.

5. Humidity

A humid gas is described by four related quantities. The definitions and the arithmetic are the same as in the dedicated humidity chapter; what follows is the compressed version needed for the dryer calculations.

QuantityDefinition
Absolute humidity Y(Mv/Mg) pv/(P − pv); 0.622 for water in air
Relative humidity RHpv/psat(T)
Percent saturation SY/Ysat(T, P); always smaller than RH
Dew pointthe temperature at which psat equals the actual pv
Wet-bulb temperaturethe temperature reached by a small liquid surface evaporating into the gas; the constant-wet-bulb line is the adiabatic cooling path

Three relations drive every calculation:

cs = 0.24 + 0.45 Y (Btu/lb·°F), or 1.006 + 1.84 Y (kJ/kg·K)
H = 0.24 T + Y ( 1 061 + 0.45 T ) (Btu/lb, T in °F)
vH = 0.0252 TR ( 1 + 1.607 Y ) (ft³/lb dry air)

The adiabatic-saturation line is what connects the inlet and exit of a dryer:

Y = Ysat(Twb) − csλwb ( T − Twb )

Heating at constant total pressure leaves Y and pv unchanged and lowers both RH and S. Cooling above the dew point leaves Y unchanged and raises both RH and S; below the dew point, condensation begins and Y falls along the saturation curve.

Worked 5.3: condensation on cooling and on compression

Show solution

Concept discourse: S is always smaller than RHThe two ratios share the same measured Y but different denominators:
S = RH × P − psatP − pv
Since pv < psat for any unsaturated mixture, the correction factor is less than 1, so S < RH. At low humidity the two are close; at high humidity and low total pressure they diverge. The one-line check — if your S exceeds your RH, swap them — catches the error in every CO2–water and air–water problem in this chapter.
