1. Limits

The limit is the foundation of every derivative in this chapter. It is the value a function approaches as its input approaches some target, and it need not equal the function's value at that target.

limx→a f(x) = L

The rigorous statement is the epsilon-delta definition: for every ε > 0 there is a δ > 0 such that |f(x) − L| < ε whenever 0 < |x − a| < δ. Every other limit technique — factoring, L'Hôpital, series — is a shortcut past this definition.

The determinate and indeterminate cases

Substitution is the first move. If it produces a finite value, that is the limit. If it produces one of the seven indeterminate forms — 0/0, ∞/∞, 0·∞, ∞ − ∞, 0⁰, 1∞, ∞⁰ — a further technique is required:

Indeterminate formTechnique
0/0, ∞/∞Factor and cancel, or L'Hôpital's rule
0·∞Rewrite as a ratio, then L'Hôpital
∞ − ∞Combine over a common denominator or rationalize
1∞Take logarithms, or use lim(1 + a/n)n = ea
0⁰, ∞⁰Take logarithms and use L'Hôpital on the exponent

Limits at infinity

For a rational function of two polynomials, the limit at infinity is decided by degrees:

Degree of numerator vs denominatorLimit as x → ∞
deg(num) < deg(den)0
deg(num) = deg(den)ratio of leading coefficients
deg(num) > deg(den)∞ (or −∞ depending on signs)

For a difference involving a square root, the conjugate multiplication is the standard technique. For x → −∞, note that √x² = |x| = −x, because x is negative: the sign of the leading term on exit is the trap.

Continuity and discontinuity

A function is continuous at a if three things hold: f(a) is defined, the limit exists, and the limit equals f(a). When one of these fails, the discontinuity is:

TypeDiagnosisExample behaviour
RemovableLimit exists but f(a) is undefined or does not equal the limithole in the graph
JumpLeft and right limits exist but differstep in the graph
Infinite (essential)Function grows without boundvertical asymptote

“Essential discontinuity” is a synonym for infinite discontinuity in the older literature, and it is that synonym that the exam expects. A removable discontinuity is what a factor that cancels leaves behind; an infinite discontinuity is what a surviving zero in the denominator leaves behind.

Removable discontinuity
A factor that cancels leaves a removable discontinuity (a hole). A factor in the denominator that does not cancel would leave an infinite discontinuity. A jump discontinuity requires left and right limits that differ, which is not the case here.

2. Angle Trigonometry

The derivatives of the trigonometric functions are built from one fundamental limit and a family of sign rules. High-order derivatives follow a periodic pattern that makes them easy to compute once the pattern is identified.

Derivatives of the six functions

d/dx sin x = cos x
d/dx cos x = −sin x
d/dx tan x = sec² x
d/dx cot x = −csc² x
d/dx sec x = sec x tan x
d/dx csc x = −csc x cot x

Two things carry the whole table: the minus sign on cosine (and on cotangent and cosecant, which derive from cosine), and the sec² for tangent. Every other rule is a consequence of those two.

High-order derivatives

Repeated differentiation of sin or cos cycles with period 4:

d/dx sin x = cos x, d²/dx² sin x = −sin x, d³/dx³ sin x = −cos x, d⁴/dx⁴ sin x = sin x

For a function of the form f(x) = a sin x + b cos x, the n-th derivative follows the rule

dn/dxn sin x = sin( x + nπ/2 )
dn/dxn cos x = cos( x + nπ/2 )

Equivalently, reduce n mod 4 to find which of the four forms applies. For n = 999, 999 mod 4 = 3, so the derivative is the third-derivative form.

n mod 4dn/dxn sin xdn/dxn cos x
0sin xcos x
1cos x−sin x
2−sin x−cos x
3−cos xsin x

3. Slope of a Line

The slope of a curve at a point is the derivative of y with respect to x at that point. For a curve given in implicit form, the derivative is found by implicit differentiation.

Implicit differentiation

Differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x (so every derivative of y brings a factor of y′), then solve for y′:

d/dx [ yn ] = n yn−1 y′
d/dx [ xy ] = y + x y′

The tangent-slope formula

After differentiating, substitute the point (x₀, y₀) into the resulting expression for y′. The number that comes out is the slope of the tangent line at that point. If the question asks for the slope of the normal, the answer is the negative reciprocal of the tangent slope:

mnormal = −1mtangent

Applying it to a circle

A circle x² + y² − 2ax − 2by + c = 0 has derivative

2x + 2y y′ − 2a − 2b y′ = 0
y′ = a − xy − b

which is the slope of the tangent at (x, y). Substituting the point gives the numerical answer.

Worked 3.1: slope of a circle at a point

Find the slope of the curve x² + y² − 6x + 10y + 5 = 0 at the point (1, 0).

Show solution
Step 1 — differentiate implicitly, remembering that y is a function of x
2x + 2y y′ − 6 + 10 y′ = 0
Step 2 — collect the y′ terms
y′ ( 2y + 10 ) = 6 − 2x
Step 3 — solve for y′
y′ = 6 − 2x2y + 10
Step 4 — substitute the point (1, 0)
y′ = 6 − 2(1)2(0) + 10 = 410
slope = 2/5
The derivative of −6x is −6, not −6x, because it is a first-power term. The derivative of +10y is +10y′, because y is a function of x. Both are implicit-differentiation basics, and both are frequently missed when the problem is done in a hurry.
Variables x, y — the coordinates of a point on the curve y′ — the derivative dy/dx, the slope of the tangent

4. Graph

Curve sketching turns the derivatives into geometric information. The first derivative controls where the function rises and falls and where its peaks and valleys lie; the second derivative controls the curvature and the points where the concavity flips.

Sign of the first derivative

f′(x)Behaviour of f
f′ > 0increasing
f′ < 0decreasing
f′ = 0critical point (possible max, min, or flat inflection)

Sign of the second derivative

f″(x)Concavity
f″ > 0concave up
f″ < 0concave down
f″ = 0 and changes signpoint of inflection

The second derivative decides the nature of a critical point in one line:

f′(c) = 0 and f″(c) > 0 ⇒ relative minimum at c
f′(c) = 0 and f″(c) < 0 ⇒ relative maximum at c
f′(c) = 0 and f″(c) = 0 ⇒ inconclusive; fall back to the first-derivative test

Working example family: cubic polynomials

For a cubic f(x) = ax³/3 + bx² + cx + d (a > 0), the classic three questions are: where is f increasing, where is f decreasing, and where is f concave up or down?

  • The critical points are the roots of f′(x) = ax² + 2bx + c.
  • The increasing intervals are those on which f′ > 0 (outside the interval between the roots, for a > 0).
  • The decreasing interval is between the roots.
  • The inflection point is where f″ = 2ax + 2b = 0, i.e. at x = −b/a.

5. Minimum/Maximum

An optimization problem asks for the extreme value of a quantity subject to a constraint. The workflow is fixed:

  • Write the quantity to be optimized as a function of a single variable using the constraint.
  • Differentiate and set the derivative to zero.
  • Check that the result is a maximum or minimum (second-derivative test or physical argument).
  • Substitute back to find the other quantities the question asks for.

The critical-point conditions

f′(c) = 0 with f″(c) > 0 ⇒ local minimum
f′(c) = 0 with f″(c) < 0 ⇒ local maximum
f′(c) = 0 with f″(c) = 0 ⇒ test fails; use the first-derivative test

Two classic patterns

Split a number into two parts. If x is one part, the other is N − x, and the product (N − x)·x² is maximized by differentiating:

d/dx [ x² ( N − x ) ] = 2Nx − 3x² = x ( 2N − 3x )

The positive root x = 2N/3 gives the maximum; the other part is N/3. For N = 120, the parts are 80 and 40.

Agency pricing. Revenue = (price per person) × (number of persons). When the price declines linearly with the number above a base, the revenue becomes a quadratic in the number, and its vertex gives the maximum. For a base of n₀ at price p₀ with a reduction of d per person above n₀, the revenue is

R(n) = ( p₀ − d( n − n₀ ) ) · n

Differentiate and solve; the optimal n is (p₀ + d n₀)/(2d).

Boxes, cylinders and distances

Closed cylinder of given volume V. Surface area A = 2πr² + 2πrh, with h = V/(πr²). Substituting and differentiating gives r³ = V/(2π), h = 2r. The minimum A is then 6πr².

Distance from a point to a curve. Minimize the square of the distance (D² instead of D), then square root at the end. This avoids the messy differentiation of a square root.

6. Circumscribed Polygons (Inscribed Figures)

A solid inscribed in another solid touches the outer solid at a prescribed set of points, and one of its dimensions is set by that contact. The optimization then asks for the maximum or minimum volume, surface, or dimension.

Reading the constraint from the geometry

Every inscribed-solid problem reduces to one relation between the inner dimensions and the outer ones. The three standard relations are:

Outer shapeInner shapeContact relation
Cone (height H, radius R)Cylinder (radius r, height h) inscribed with coaxial axisBy similar triangles: hH = R − rR
Sphere (radius R)Cylinder (radius r, height h) inscribedHalf-height and radius form a right triangle with R: r² + (h/2)² = R²
Sphere (radius R)Cone (height h, radius r) inscribedh² − 2Rh + r² = 0

Ratio results worth memorizing

The following ratios recur so often that they are worth committing to memory:

max cylinder in a cone: r = (2/3) R, h = H/3, Vcyl/Vcone = 4/9 ≈ 44.4 %
max cylinder in a sphere: r = √(2/3) R, h = 2R/√3, Vcyl = (4π/(3√3)) R³
max cone in a sphere: h = (4/3) R, r = (2√2/3) R, Vcone = (32π/81) R³

The cone-in-sphere result is the one that catches people, because the "obvious" guess h = 2R (the full diameter) is not the maximum; h = 4R/3 is.

The derivative step

After substituting the constraint, the objective becomes a function of one variable, and the derivative is set to zero in the usual way. Many of these derivatives reduce to a simple algebra equation (a quadratic or a linear in the variable of interest), so the arithmetic is often lighter than the geometry.

7. Trigonometric Leveling

A sighting problem in calculus: two angles of elevation from a distance, or two angles of depression from a height, reduce to a single triangle in which one distance or angle is optimized.

Two classic configurations

Statue on a base — maximizing the subtended angle. An observer at horizontal distance x from the base of a tower sees the top at angle arctan(htop/x) and the base at arctan(hbase/x). The angle subtended by the object of interest is the difference of those two angles:

θ( x ) = arctan( htopx ) − arctan( hbasex )

Differentiating and setting dθ/dx = 0 gives

x² = htop · hbase ⇒ x = √( htop · hbase )

That is the geometric mean of the two heights, and it is the answer to the classic "where should the observer stand" problem.

The shortest ladder over a wall. A wall of height h at distance d from a building; a ladder of length L leans on the wall and reaches the building. If θ is the angle the ladder makes with the ground,

L( θ ) = hsin θ + dcos θ

Minimizing gives tan³θ = h/d, and the minimum length follows from Lmin = ( h2/3 + d2/3 )3/2.

Worked 7.1: statue on a base
