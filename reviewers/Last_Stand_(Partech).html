1. Particle Classification

Particles are described by three quantities that are often confused with one another.

  • Size. The nominal diameter, often the equivalent-volume spherical diameter. For fine particles the size may be measured by sieving, sedimentation, or light scattering; for ultrafine particles the appropriate measure is the specific surface area per unit mass, because the particle is too small for gravitational sedimentation and its behaviour is dominated by its surface.
  • Shape. The sphericity φ measures how closely the particle approaches a sphere. It is defined as the ratio of the surface area of the equivalent-volume sphere to the actual surface area of the particle:
    φ = AsphereAparticle, Asphere = π deq²
    where deq is the diameter of the sphere with the same volume as the particle.
  • Equivalent diameter. For a non-spherical particle, deq is the diameter of the sphere with the same volume, so that
    Vparticle = π deq³ / 6

Two size classes of equipment are distinguished by the amount of solids they handle. A classifier separates a particle mixture into two fractions by size, usually with a substantial solids load. A clarifier removes a trace of solids from a liquid, producing a clear liquid as the product. The distinction is one of scale and intent: classifiers divide a substantial feed, clarifiers polish a dilute stream.

Concept discourse: why ultrafine particles are measured by surface areaFor particles above roughly 1 µm, sedimentation and sieving give a reproducible size, because the behaviour of the particle is dominated by its mass and its size. Below about 1 µm, Brownian motion competes with gravity, sedimentation becomes impractically slow, and sieves cannot be manufactured fine enough. The quantity that still characterises the particle is its specific surface area per unit mass: for a given mass of material, the finer the particles, the greater the total exposed area. That is why the answer to "size measurement of ultrafine particles" is surface area per unit mass and not micron.

2. Sieving

A sieve is described by its mesh number, which is the number of openings per linear inch of screen (the Tyler standard, used in most US practice). Larger mesh numbers correspond to smaller openings. The opening of a 200-mesh Tyler screen is 0.0074 cm, or 74 µm — a number worth memorising because it appears in many exam questions.

The screen effectiveness measures how well the screen separates a feed into an oversize product (coarse) and an undersize product (fine). For a feed in which xF is the mass fraction of oversize material, xO the fraction in the oversize product, and xU the fraction in the undersize product,

E = ( xO − xF )( xF − xU )xF ( 1 − xF )( xO − xU )

The formula can be understood as the product of two recoveries:

E = ( oversize in Ooversize in F ) × ( undersize in Uundersize in F )

Both approaches give the same number, and doing both is a good check. A perfect screen has E = 1; a screen that passes everything has E = 0.

A common convention confusion: some textbooks call the oversize stream "rejects" and the undersize stream "accepts", and use the formula in terms of those fractions. The labels do not change the arithmetic — only which fraction is called x needs to be stated explicitly.

3. Size Reduction

Size reduction (crushing and grinding) is followed by screening to return the oversize material to the crusher. This recycle loop is the circulating load, and its analysis is the classic mass-balance problem of the chapter.

For a single crusher with a screen, the terminology is:

  • Fresh feed: the new material entering the loop from upstream.
  • Product: the final undersize material leaving the loop.
  • Oversize (circulating load): the material retained on the screen and returned to the crusher.

The steady-state balances on the crusher loop are:

total load = fresh feed + circulating load
product = fresh feed (by mass, at steady state)

The circulation ratio is the circulating load divided by the product. A high ratio means the crusher is being fed a lot of material that has already passed through the screen — a sign that the crusher is not reducing the material efficiently, or that the screen is cutting at a size the crusher cannot easily achieve.

The screen analysis is expressed as a cumulative or differential distribution over the sieve sizes. The mass fractions in each size class are given for the feed, the oversize and the undersize. From these the circulating load follows by a component balance on one size class.

Concept discourse: the circulation ratio in a closed grinding circuitIn a closed-circuit grinding mill the material leaving the mill goes to a classifier, which returns the coarse fraction to the mill and sends the fines forward as product. The circulating load is the amount of material that goes round the loop more than once. A high circulating load means the mill is being fed a lot of material it has already ground — which is inefficient. The circulation ratio is a design quantity: too low means the mill is underloaded, too high means it is overloaded. The optimal value balances the two.

4. Particle Settling

A single particle in a fluid reaches a terminal velocity when drag equals the buoyant-corrected weight:

vt = √4 g d ( ρs − ρ )3 CD ρ

with the drag coefficient CD a function of the Reynolds number Re = ρ vt d/μ. In the Stokes regime (Re < 0.3) the drag is viscous and the terminal velocity simplifies to

vt = g d² ( ρs − ρ )18 μ

Beyond Stokes, the velocity must be found by iterating: guess vt, compute Re and CD, recompute vt, repeat. Two or three passes usually close the calculation.

For a concentrated suspension the settling velocity is reduced by the presence of the other particles. The Richardson–Zaki equation relates the hindered settling velocity to the voidage:

vh = vt εn (or equivalently vh = vt ( 1 − φ )n )

where ε is the liquid volume fraction, φ = 1 − ε is the solids volume fraction, and n is the R–Z index (about 4.65 for Re < 0.2 and 2.4 for Re > 500). The velocities are always measured in the same unit and the ratio vh/vt is always less than 1.

5. Fluidization

A bed of solid particles becomes fluidized when the upward gas velocity is just large enough that the drag force on the particles equals their buoyant-corrected weight. The three quantities that define the bed are the minimum fluidization velocity umf, the pressure drop at minimum fluidization ΔP, and the bed height Lmf.

Pressure drop

At minimum fluidization the pressure drop across the bed equals the weight of the bed per unit area:

ΔP = ( 1 − εmf ) ( ρs − ρg ) g Lmf

Bed height

The mass of solids is conserved, so the bed height follows from the solids inventory:

Lmf = msρs Ac ( 1 − εmf )

Minimum fluidization velocity

The Ergun equation at minimum fluidization is

150 μ ( 1 − εmf )φ² dp² εmf³ umf + 1.75 ρgφ dp εmf³ umf² = ( ρs − ρg ) g

For fine particles the first (viscous) term dominates and the equation is linear in umf; for coarse particles the second (inertial) term dominates and the equation is quadratic. Solving requires either algebra (when one term is negligible) or trial and error.

6. Centrifugation

A centrifuge separates particles by the centrifugal force, which is many times gravity. For two geometrically similar centrifuges to give the same separation performance, the centrifugal acceleration at the corresponding radius must be the same:

ω1² R1 = ω2² R2

Since ω = 2πN (with N in revolutions per second, i.e., Hz), this becomes

N1² D1 = N2² D2

so the scale-up relation for equal performance is

N2 = N1 √( D1 / D2 )

The centrifugal force factor (or relative centrifugal force, RCF) is the ratio of the centrifugal acceleration to gravitational acceleration:

RCF = ω² Rg = ( 2 π N )² Rg

A bench centrifuge running at a few thousand rpm can generate an RCF of several thousand g; an industrial decanter at a few thousand rpm on a 1 m bowl generates much less per unit radius but processes far more volume. The scale-up from laboratory to plant therefore requires computing N2 from the equal-RCF condition, not from the equal-rpm condition.

Worked 6.1: scale-up of a centrifuge

A centrifuge of 0.9 m diameter rotates at 20 Hz. At what speed should a laboratory centrifuge of 150 mm diameter be run to duplicate its performance?

Show solution
Step 1 — equal centrifugal acceleration at the corresponding radius
ω1² R1 = ω2² R2
Step 2 — replace ω by 2πN and R by D/2; the 2π and the 1/2 cancel on both sides
N1² D1 = N2² D2
Step 3 — substitute the values
D1 = 0.9 m, N1 = 20 Hz, D2 = 0.15 m
N2² = N1² D1D2 = 400 × 0.90.15 = 3600.15 = 2 400
Step 4 — take the square root
N2 = √2 400 = 49 Hz
The laboratory centrifuge must run at 49 Hz (about 2 940 rpm) — more than twice the speed of the large unit — to duplicate the performance of the 0.9 m machine. The answer comes from the square root of the diameter ratio; skipping the square root gives 20 × 6 = 120 Hz, which is four times too fast.
Variables N1, N2 — rotational speeds, Hz D1, D2 — bowl diameters, m ω1, ω2 — angular velocities, rad/s R1, R2 — bowl radii, m
Concept discourse: what “duplicate the performance” actually meansThe condition that two centrifuges give the same separation is not that they run at the same rpm, nor that they produce the same RCF. It is that the settling velocity of a particle relative to the bowl is the same. In the Stokes regime the settling velocity is proportional to the centrifugal acceleration ω²R, so equal performance requires ω1²R1 = ω2²R2. In the turbulent regime the settling velocity is proportional to √(ω²R), which would change the scale-up relation to ω1²R1 being proportional to the fourth power. Industrial centrifuges are usually in the Stokes regime for the particles of interest, so the square-root scale-up is the standard one.
